Appendix C: Capstone Projects

Choose one project for week 16, or take all four in turn afterward. Each is scoped at 15 to 25 hours and draws on several chapters. Each asks for a short written report, because being able to state what you measured and how sure you are is part of the skill.

A rule for all four: decide the success criteria and write them down before running anything.

Project 1: a language model, properly

Goal. Rebuild the Chapter 12 model as a small but complete modern language model, and characterize how it scales.

Draws on. Chapters 6, 7, 9, 11, 12, 13, 15.

Required.

  1. A byte-pair tokenizer trained on your corpus, with a vocabulary of 1,000 to 4,000. Use a corpus of 10 to 100 megabytes of text you find interesting.

  2. Rotary position encoding in place of the learned table.

  3. A training loop with warmup, cosine decay, clipping, mixed precision if you have a GPU, and checkpoints you can resume from.

  4. Generation with a key-value cache, temperature, and top-p.

  5. A scaling study: at least five model sizes spanning a factor of 30 in parameters, each trained on the same number of tokens.

Success criteria.

  • Initial loss within 5 percent of log V, and one batch overfit to near zero, before any long run.

  • Held-out bits per byte reported for every size, with a fitted power law and its exponent.

  • Cached and uncached generation produce identical output.

Stretch. Fix a compute budget and find the best split between model size and training tokens. Compare your tokens-per-parameter optimum with Section 13.4.

Report. Two pages: architecture, the scaling plot, three samples from the largest model, and what limited you.

Project 2: reverse-engineer a small network

Goal. Train a small Transformer on an algorithmic task, show that it generalizes, and explain how it computes the answer.

Draws on. Chapters 5, 7, 11, 19, 20.

Required.

  1. Choose a task. Two good options: addition modulo a prime, trained on a fraction of all pairs; or sequences of random tokens followed by their repetition, as in Problem 19.7.

  2. Train a one- or two-layer model. Plot training and test accuracy against step on a logarithmic axis.

  3. Probe the residual stream for the quantities you think the algorithm needs, with a control on an untrained model.

  4. Ablate every head and every MLP layer, using mean ablation, and rank them.

  5. Use activation patching between pairs of inputs that differ in one controlled way to locate where the relevant information moves.

Success criteria.

  • Test accuracy above 99 percent on held-out inputs.

  • A written account of the algorithm, specific enough that a reader could predict the effect of an intervention you have not yet run.

  • At least one such prediction, made in writing and then tested.

Stretch. For modular addition, apply a Fourier transform to the embedding matrix and compare with the published mechanism. For the repetition task, identify the previous-token head and the induction head.

Report. A diagram of the circuit, the evidence for each claim, and what remains unexplained.

Project 3: a retrieval-augmented assistant, evaluated

Goal. Build question answering over a collection of your own documents and measure it honestly.

Draws on. Chapters 15, 16, 17.

Required.

  1. A corpus of at least 200 pages you know well enough to write questions about.

  2. An evaluation set of at least 50 questions, each with a reference answer and the passage that supports it. Write these before building anything. Include ten questions the corpus cannot answer.

  3. A retriever, evaluated alone by recall at k, with an ablation over chunk size.

  4. A generator, any LLM you can run or call, with a prompt that requires citations and permits "not in the documents".

  5. End-to-end evaluation: answer accuracy with a bootstrap interval, the rate of unsupported claims, and behavior on the unanswerable questions.

  6. A prompt-injection test: plant an instruction inside one document and record what happens.

Success criteria.

  • Retrieval and generation errors reported separately, so that every failure is assigned to a stage.

  • Every headline number carries an interval.

  • Ten failures analyzed individually.

Stretch. Add a reranker or hybrid retrieval and test whether the improvement is statistically distinguishable from zero on your set.

Report. The evaluation design, the results table, the failure analysis, and one paragraph on whether you would trust the system and for what.

Project 4: adapt and compress

Goal. Take a small open-weight language model, adapt it to a task, shrink it, and quantify every trade-off.

Draws on. Chapters 14, 15, 17.

Required.

  1. A task with an objective metric: classification, extraction into a fixed schema, or code that must pass tests. Hold out a test set and do not look at it until the end.

  2. A baseline: the unmodified model with your best prompt, including few-shot examples.

  3. LoRA fine-tuning at three ranks. Report accuracy against trainable parameters.

  4. A forgetting check: performance on an unrelated task before and after.

  5. Quantization of the adapted model to 8 and 4 bits. Report accuracy, memory, and tokens per second for each.

Success criteria.

  • A paired comparison of the fine-tuned model against the prompted baseline, with an interval for the difference.

  • A single table of accuracy, memory, and speed across all variants.

  • A stated recommendation for which variant to deploy under a memory limit you choose, with the reasoning.

Stretch. Distill the adapted model into a smaller one, and place it in the same table.

Report. The table, the forgetting result, and a paragraph on when fine-tuning was worth it compared with prompting.
