4.1 The artificial neuron

A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and applies a scalar function φ:

a \;=\; \varphi\big(w^{\top}x + b\big).

The quantity z = wᵀx + b is the pre-activation and a is the activation. Geometrically, z is the signed distance of x from the hyperplane wᵀx + b = 0, scaled by ‖w‖. The neuron reports, through φ, how far the input lies on one side of a plane.

The name comes from a loose analogy with biological neurons, which integrate inputs and fire past a threshold. The analogy motivated the early work and explains the vocabulary. It should not be leaned on: nothing later in this book depends on resemblance to a brain.

4.2 The perceptron

Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) is a neuron with a hard threshold. With labels y ∈ {−1, +1}, it predicts sign(wᵀx + b). Its learning rule is the simplest imaginable. Visit the examples in turn. When one is misclassified, nudge the plane toward classifying it correctly:

w \leftarrow w + y\,x, \qquad b \leftarrow b + y .

The perceptron convergence theorem says that if the data can be separated by some hyperplane with margin γ, and all inputs have norm at most R, the rule makes at most (R/γ)² mistakes and then stops, having found a separator (Problem 4.3). This was the first learning guarantee for a machine, and it produced great optimism.

The limitation. The guarantee requires separability. Consider XOR on two binary inputs: output 1 when exactly one input is 1. Suppose a threshold unit computed it, firing when w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b > 0. The four cases require

b \le 0, \qquad w_1 + b > 0, \qquad w_2 + b > 0, \qquad w_1 + w_2 + b \le 0 .

Adding the middle two gives w₁ + w₂ + 2b > 0, so w₁ + w₂ + b > −b ≥ 0, contradicting the last. No single neuron computes XOR. Minsky and Papert made this point in 1969, and since nobody then knew how to train anything deeper, research on neural networks nearly stopped for over a decade.

4.3 Hidden layers

The fix is to compute intermediate features first. Use the rectified linear unit, ReLU(z) = max(0, z), and two hidden neurons:

h_1 = \mathrm{ReLU}(x_1 + x_2), \qquad h_2 = \mathrm{ReLU}(x_1 + x_2 - 1), \qquad y = h_1 - 2\,h_2 .

Look at what the hidden layer did. In the input plane the two classes cannot be split by a line. In the (h₁, h₂) plane the points are (0, 0), (1, 0), (1, 0), and (2, 1), and the output y = h₁ − 2h₂ is a linear function that gets all four right. The hidden layer re-represented the input so that a linear model suffices.

This is the whole idea of deep learning in four data points. Layers before the last transform the data into a representation in which the problem is easy. The last layer solves the easy problem. Here the representation was designed by hand; from Chapter 5 on it will be learned.

4.4 The multilayer perceptron

A multilayer perceptron (MLP) alternates affine maps with an elementwise nonlinearity. With input h^(0) = x and L layers:

z^{(l)} = W^{(l)} h^{(l-1)} + b^{(l)}, \qquad h^{(l)} = \varphi\big(z^{(l)}\big), \qquad l = 1, \dots, L-1,
\hat y \;=\; z^{(L)} = W^{(L)} h^{(L-1)} + b^{(L)} .

If layer l has n_l units, then W^(l) ∈ R^(n_l × n_(l−1)) and b^(l) ∈ R^(n_l). The number of units per layer is the width; the number of layers is the depth. The layers between input and output are hidden. The final layer usually has no nonlinearity: its outputs are logits or regression values, to be fed to the loss.

Figure 4.2: each bundle of lines between two columns is one weight matrix, with one entry per line. Here the three matrices hold 12, 16, and 8 weights.

Parameters. Layer l contributes n_l · n_(l−1) weights and n_l biases. Nearly all parameters of a network are in its weight matrices, and the count grows with the product of adjacent widths.

Why the nonlinearity is essential. Remove φ and compose two layers: W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = (W₂W₁)x + (W₂b₁ + b₂). That is a single affine map. By induction a linear network of any depth equals one linear layer. Depth adds expressive power only because something nonlinear sits between the matrices.

Output layers. The final layer and loss are chosen as a pair, following Chapter 3.

TaskOutputLoss
Regressionz^(L) directlyMean squared error
Binary classificationOne logit, through a sigmoidBinary cross-entropy
Classification over K classesK logits, through a softmaxCross-entropy

In each case the network up to the last hidden layer is a learned feature map, and the last layer is one of the linear models of Chapter 3 applied to those features.

The property that matters most is the derivative, because Chapter 5 will show that the gradient reaching early layers is a product of one derivative factor per layer. A sigmoid contributes at most 1/4 per layer and far less when saturated, so through ten layers the signal shrinks by a factor of a million or worse. ReLU contributes exactly 1 wherever the unit is active. Switching from sigmoid to ReLU around 2010 was one of the changes that made deep networks trainable.

Use ReLU as the default for MLPs and convolutional networks and GELU for Transformers. The differences among the modern choices are small.

4.6 What a network can represent

Universal approximation theorem. Let φ be continuous and not a polynomial. For any continuous function f on a compact subset of R^n and any ε > 0, there is a network with one hidden layer and finitely many units whose output is within ε of f everywhere on that set. (Cybenko 1989 for sigmoids; Hornik 1991; Leshno and others 1993 for the general condition.)

Why it is plausible, in one dimension. A sum of k ReLU units with different offsets is a continuous piecewise-linear function with up to k kinks, and you can place the kinks and choose the slopes freely. Any continuous function on an interval can be approximated by piecewise-linear interpolation with enough knots. So a wide enough single hidden layer approximates any continuous function of one variable.

What the theorem does not say.

  1. It does not bound the width. For functions of many variables the required number of units can grow exponentially with dimension.

  2. It does not say gradient descent will find the approximating weights. Existence is not learnability.

  3. It does not say the network found from finite data will be close to f away from the data. It is about representation, not generalization.

The theorem removes one worry, that the family might be too small. The three hard questions of Chapter 1 are untouched by it.

4.7 Why depth

If one hidden layer suffices in principle, why are useful networks deep? Because depth composes, and composition can be exponentially more efficient than breadth.

A concrete case: the tent map g(x) = 2 ReLU(x) − 4 ReLU(x − ½) on [0, 1] rises from 0 to 1 and falls back. It takes two ReLU units. Composing it with itself k times uses 2k units in k layers and produces a sawtooth with 2^(k−1) teeth. A single hidden layer needs a unit for each kink, on the order of 2^k (Problem 4.5). More generally, the number of linear pieces a ReLU network can produce grows polynomially with width and exponentially with depth.

The intuition is reuse. A deep network computes features of features. A feature computed once at layer 3 can be used by every unit above it, where a shallow network would have to recompute it for each use. Natural data seem to have this compositional structure: edges form textures, textures form parts, parts form objects; characters form words, words form phrases.

The cost is that deep networks are harder to optimize. Chapters 5 through 7 are about paying that cost.

Lab 4: networks by hand

Verify the XOR construction.

import numpy as np

relu = lambda z: np.maximum(z, 0)
X = np.array([[0, 0], [0, 1], [1, 0], [1, 1]], dtype=float)

W1 = np.array([[1.0, 1.0],
               [1.0, 1.0]])
b1 = np.array([0.0, -1.0])
w2 = np.array([1.0, -2.0])

H = relu(X @ W1.T + b1)      # hidden features, shape (4, 2)
print(H)
print(H @ w2)                # [0. 1. 1. 0.]
