How to vote

Election Day is Tuesday, Nov. 3, 7 a.m.–7 p.m., at your assigned polling place only. Early voting is open now through Sunday, Nov. 1, 8 a.m.–8 p.m. daily, at any of Orange County's 27 sites; the closest to Mills 50 is the Marks Street Senior Recreation Complex, 99 E. Marks St.

Bring a valid, unexpired photo ID that shows your signature, such as a Florida driver license or state ID, U.S. passport, military ID or Florida concealed weapon license. If your photo ID has no signature, bring a second ID that does; with no acceptable ID you can still cast a provisional ballot.

Mail ballots can't be dropped at Election Day polling places. If you have an unreturned mail ballot, you can still vote in person instead.

Federal races

U.S. Senate (special election)

Ashley Moody (R) leads every recent public poll, by 2 to 11 points, and forecasters rate the seat Likely to Solid Republican. The winner serves the rest of Marco Rubio's term, through January 2029, and must run again in 2028. There were no debates: Moody declined, and Nixon called the refusal cowardly.

The candidates

  • Ashley Moody (R, appointed incumbent) — 51, from Plant City. Former federal prosecutor and Hillsborough circuit judge, Florida attorney general 2019–2025, appointed senator in January 2025 when Rubio became Secretary of State.

  • Angie Nixon (D) — 42, from Jacksonville. State representative since 2020, former SEIU Florida organizer, head of the voter coalition Florida For All. She joined the Democratic Socialists of America and the Working Families Party in 2026, and beat Alex Vindman 56% to 44% in August despite being heavily outspent.

  • Neil J. Gillespie (NPA) — Ocala advocate and former car dealer. Wants to bar lawyers from legislative and executive office, national health coverage with an opt-out, and secure borders with a path to citizenship. Polled at 2%.

Record and controversies

  • Moody: As attorney general she fought the 2022 and 2024 recreational-marijuana measures, joined the 2020 Texas v. Pennsylvania election suit, and kept Florida in a lawsuit seeking to strike down the ACA. A grand jury report filed under seal in January found that $10 million from a 2024 Medicaid settlement her office approved was misappropriated through the Hope Florida Foundation. As CBS News Miami described it, the report said she knew of the plan and authorized her deputy to sign; it charged no one, and she was not called to testify. Her campaign says the state health agency negotiated the settlement, and on Oct. 7 an appeals court ordered the report expunged.

  • Nixon: The Florida Trib calls her legislative record thin and says she is better known for activism. Her 2023 Gaza cease-fire resolution failed 104–2; a House ethics committee reprimanded her in May 2026 for using a megaphone during the redistricting debate, and she was arrested at a sit-in at DeSantis's office over the new congressional map. On CNN on Oct. 14 she said Moody should be locked up over Hope Florida, which Moody's campaign called a communist tactic.

Support and money: Moody is endorsed by Trump, Sen. Rick Scott, the Florida PBA and FOP, and 64 sheriffs, and had taken in about $14.5 million through Oct. 14, including transfers from allied committees. Nixon is endorsed by Sens. Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren, Reps. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Maxwell Frost, Senate Democrats' campaign arm, Giffords PAC and SEIU Florida, and had raised about $8.2 million through Sept. 30.

Polls: UNF (Oct. 12–19) Moody 49%, Nixon 45%, with Nixon up 15 among independents; co/efficient (Oct. 18–20) 48–40; Stetson (Oct. 1–9) 52–41; St. Pete Polls (Oct. 5–7) 47–45; Mason-Dixon (Sept. 28–Oct. 1) 50–40.

What's at stake: It is the sharpest ideological contrast on the ballot: a Trump-endorsed conservative with a law-enforcement résumé against a democratic-socialist organizer running on Medicare for All and a wealth tax. Polls show a closer race than the ratings imply, driven by independents, but Moody remains the favorite.

Governor and Lieutenant Governor

Byron Donalds (R) holds a narrow edge over David Jolly (D) in most October polls, from a tie to 11 points, in Florida's first open governor's race since 2018. Gov. DeSantis is term-limited, Republicans have won every governor's race since 1998, and there will be no televised debate for the first time in more than 50 years.

The governor runs the executive branch, signs or vetoes bills (including line items in the budget), and appoints judges and agency heads. Governor and lieutenant governor run as a ticket.

The main tickets

  • Byron Donalds / Bryan Avila (R) — Donalds, 47, of Naples, worked in banking and insurance, served in the Florida House 2016–2020 and in Congress since 2021, where he is a Freedom Caucus member. In January 2021 he voted to object to Arizona's and Pennsylvania's electors. He won the primary with 48% over Lt. Gov. Jay Collins (25%). Avila, 42, is an Army National Guard captain who, as a state senator, sponsored the Senate version of Amendment 3 before resigning to run.

  • David Jolly / Gwen Graham (D) — Jolly, 53, of Indian Rocks Beach, is an attorney and former lobbyist who served as a Republican congressman from Pinellas 2014–2017. He left the GOP in 2018 over Trump, became a Democrat in 2025, and won the primary with 61%. Graham is a former congresswoman, daughter of Gov. and Sen. Bob Graham, and the 2018 runner-up for governor.

Florida Cabinet

All three Republican incumbents are favored, but the CFO race is tied in recent polls and the attorney general's race ranges from a tie to a 10-point Uthmeier lead. The governor and the three Cabinet members sit together on the boards that oversee state lands, FDLE and clemency; the governor, CFO and attorney general also oversee state investments.

Attorney General

The AG is the state's chief legal officer: defends state laws in court, handles criminal appeals, pursues consumer fraud and price gouging, and oversees the statewide prosecutor. Neither nominee had a primary.

  • James Uthmeier (R, appointed incumbent) — 38. UF and Georgetown Law; DeSantis's general counsel, then chief of staff (2021–2025) and manager of his 2024 presidential campaign. Appointed AG in February 2025; this is his first campaign.

    • Record: proposed the Alligator Alcatraz detention site (since closed). A federal judge held him in contempt in June 2025 over a letter telling police he couldn't stop them from making arrests under a blocked immigration law. Sued the FDA with Texas to roll back approvals of the abortion pill mifepristone.

    • Guns: declined to defend the under-21 long-gun purchase ban and the open-carry ban; argues the felon gun ban is unconstitutional for nondangerous felons.

    • Also: a January 2026 opinion saying he won't enforce about 80 state laws he calls race-based; actions against Roblox, Meta and OpenAI; no intervention in FPL's roughly $7 billion rate increase; rewrote Amendment 3's ballot summary after a judge found the original misleading.

    • Hope Florida: $10 million from a Medicaid settlement went to the Hope Florida Foundation, and $8.5 million then passed through two groups to Keep Florida Clean, a committee tied to Uthmeier that fought the 2024 marijuana amendment. The grand jury placed him in a position of authority over the money. The grand jury charged no one; an appeals court ordered its report expunged Oct. 7, finding the settlement legal. He calls the transfers entirely legal; State Attorney Monique Worrell and the IRS are still reviewing.

    • Priorities: child predators, protecting data from China, limits on minors' social media and AI chatbots, immigration enforcement with the Trump administration.

    • Support: Trump, the Republican Attorneys General Association (seven figures), 51-plus sheriffs, Gun Owners of America. Raised $8.3 million by March 31.

    • Criticism: House Democrats sought an investigation and a special session to consider impeaching him; he also holds a $100,000-a-year UF adjunct post. The Florida Trib reported that CDR Enterprises, whose affiliates hold Alligator Alcatraz contracts, gave his political committee $250,000 in September 2025, about two weeks after an affiliate won a roughly $100 million contract to staff another detention center; the Trib alleges no direct link.

  • José Javier Rodríguez (D) — 48, of Miami. Brown and Harvard Law, Peace Corps, legal-aid lawyer on wage theft. State House 2012–2016 and Senate 2016–2020; lost re-election by 32 votes in a race tainted by a "ghost candidate" scheme that sent ex-Sen. Frank Artiles to jail. U.S. assistant secretary of labor 2024–2025.

    • Record: climate and rooftop-solar bills; a 2016 push to ease voting-rights restoration for people with felonies; a 2018 bill to loosen cash bail, which DeSantis attacked on Oct. 21 and Rodríguez calls bipartisan reform.

    • Priorities ("the people's lawyer"): investigate insurers over rate hikes and claim denials, utility rates, algorithmic rent-setting, deed theft and Big Tech harms to kids; probe no-bid Alligator Alcatraz contracts and data-center incentives; recover the Hope Florida $10 million; oppose selling conservation land; defend home rule.

    • Support: former AG Bob Butterworth, Florida AFL-CIO, AFSCME, Latino Victory Fund, Rep. Maxwell Frost, former Sen. Bill Nelson, and a former GOP rival, Steven Leskovich. Raised just over $1 million by March 31.

    • His positions on abortion, immigration enforcement and Amendment 3 were not found in the sources reviewed.

Polls: JMI/Targoz (Sept. 28–Oct. 4) Uthmeier 50%, Rodríguez 40%; St. Pete Polls (Oct. 5–7) 45–43; Change Research (Sept. 7–9) 46–46.

What's at stake: This race is largely a verdict on Uthmeier's aggressive use of the office on immigration, guns, tech and culture-war issues, plus the Hope Florida affair. Rodríguez pitches a shift toward consumer protection against insurers and utilities.

Chief Financial Officer

The CFO runs the Department of Financial Services: pays and audits state accounts, helps insurance consumers, investigates insurance fraud and serves as State Fire Marshal. Insurance rates are set by a separate regulator overseen by the governor and Cabinet.

  • Blaise Ingoglia (R, appointed incumbent) — 55, of Spring Hill. Homebuilder and mortgage-company founder; state House 2014–2022, state GOP chair 2015–2019, state Senate 2022–2025. Appointed by DeSantis in July 2025; won the primary 61% to 39%.

    • Record: leads "Florida DOGE" audits of local-government spending. He claimed nearly $200 million in Jacksonville overspending; a January report detailed about $95 million in examples. He criticized Orlando's spending on a tree inventory and immigrant legal aid and Orange County's on LGBTQ youth services; Mayor Dyer defended the city's budgets.

    • Positions: campaigning for Amendment 3; wants homestead taxes eliminated; proposed requiring searchable local budgets, salaries and contracts online.

    • Support: Sen. Moody, former CFOs Atwater, Gallagher and Patronis, Senate President Albritton, 60 sheriffs.

    • Criticism: armed department agents questioned a 77-year-old man over a postcard criticizing Ingoglia (his office cited a threat assessment); Florida Bulldog reported his disclosure left out about 112 company-held properties, which his office disputes; critics including former CFO Alex Sink call the audits political.

  • Annette Taddeo (D) — 59, of Miami. Born in Colombia, came to the U.S. at 17 after FARC guerrillas kidnapped her father; founded a translation firm; 2014 lieutenant governor nominee; state Senate 2017–2022 on the Banking and Insurance Committee. Won the primary 66% to 34%.

    • Positions: an independent watchdog rather than a governor's ally; tougher insurer oversight and prompt claims; human review and appeals when insurers use AI to decide claims; audits of no-bid contracts such as Alligator Alcatraz. On Amendment 3 she says homeowners need relief but questions the rushed process and warns costs could shift elsewhere.

    • Support: former CFO Alex Sink, Orange County Mayor Jerry Demings, Florida AFL-CIO, the Teamsters.

    • Money: far behind; about $175,000 in her campaign account and $400,000 in her committee in early October, plus public matching funds.

Polls: St. Pete Polls (Oct. 5–7) Ingoglia 44%, Taddeo 42%; JMI/Targoz 43–43; two September polls tied.

What's at stake: The closest Cabinet race. It is partly a referendum on DOGE-style audits of local governments, including Orlando and Orange County, and on Amendment 3, which Ingoglia is actively promoting.

Commissioner of Agriculture

The commissioner runs the Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services: food safety, crop and livestock protection, consumer complaints, gas-pump and ride inspections, state forests and wildfires, concealed-weapon licenses and hemp rules.

  • Wilton Simpson (R, incumbent) — 60, of Trilby. Egg farmer; state Senate 2012–2022, including Senate President; elected commissioner in 2022 with 59%. Won the primary 69% to 31%.

    • Record: 2021 Wildlife Corridor Act; a law shielding sugar growers from lawsuits over field burning; as commissioner, bans on lab-grown meat and on adding fluoride to public water, limits on land buys by "hostile" foreign nations, and sweeps against THC products that appeal to children.

    • Support: Trump, DeSantis, the Florida Chamber, 61 sheriffs; $1.1 million in his campaign plus more than $25 million in committees.

    • Criticism: a ranch gave $100,000 months after a roughly $10 million state conservation deal, which Integrity Florida said looks like pay-to-play though it is legal; critics say his easement program pulled money from Florida Forever and doesn't require public access.

  • Joey Mendoza Atkins (D) — about 40. Trial lawyer who has done legal aid for migrant farmworkers and DACA recipients; lost a 2024 congressional race. Won the primary 60% to 40%.

    • Positions: a data-center moratorium, suspending the state gas tax, repealing the sugar growers' lawsuit shield, opposing wider E-Verify, legalizing cannabis, and judging conservation by public benefit rather than acres.

    • Money: about $1,700 in contributions plus $8,500 he lent himself, as of mid-August.

  • Write-in: Kyle "KC" Gibson qualified; his name isn't printed, but there is a write-in line.

Poll: JMI/Targoz (Sept. 28–Oct. 4) Simpson 50%, Atkins 39%.

What's at stake: A low-profile race with a huge money gap. The real differences are data centers, cannabis, sugar-field burning and how conservation money is spent.

State Legislature

Florida House, District 42 (open seat)

Felipe Sousa Lazaballet (D) and Bonnie Jackson (R) are running to replace term-limited Rep. Anna Eskamani in a district Democrats have carried by about 15 points in each of the last two elections. District 42 runs from just north of Maitland to Belle Isle, including Mills 50, and neither nominee had a primary.

  • Felipe Sousa Lazaballet (D) — 40, executive director of Hope CommUnity Center in Apopka. Born in Brazil, he was undocumented for years, became a citizen in 2021, and joined 2010's Trail of Dreams walk for immigrant rights. He later worked for the City of Orlando on Pulse recovery.

    • Positions: use the Sadowski trust fund for housing, renters' rights, insurance reform and transit funding; opposes Amendment 3; full school funding and higher teacher pay; opposes vouchers; Medicaid expansion; protect rural boundaries; keep school vaccine requirements; opposes restrictive abortion laws.

    • Support: Eskamani; Reps. Maxwell Frost and Sarah McBride; Mayor Buddy Dyer; State Attorney Monique Worrell; Equality Florida (LGBTQ rights); Florida AFL-CIO; SEIU; the Orange County teachers union; Sierra Club; firefighters' unions; Florida Medical Association. Raised more than $200,000 by May.

  • Bonnie Jackson (R) — 63, Winter Park attorney since 1995 and a former Harris County, Texas prosecutor; co-founded Jackson Law International; former Guardian ad Litem. She was the 2022 Republican nominee against Eskamani and lost 57% to 42%.

    • Positions: supports eliminating property taxes; faster permitting and fixing Live Local Act housing funding; letting tourist-tax money pay for transportation, infrastructure and public safety; limits on AI, including keeping companion chatbots away from children; water quality. Says abortion isn't on the ballot; backs current immigration enforcement; says vaccine research deserves more study; on home rule and rural boundaries, says it depends.

    • Support: Sen. Jason Brodeur; Reps. Doug Bankson, Erika Booth, Rachel Plakon, Susan Plasencia and Paula Stark; Florida Fraternal Order of Police; Florida Family Action (social-conservative); Republican National Hispanic Assembly. Fundraising totals not found.

Judges

Florida Supreme Court: retain Justice Carlos G. Muñiz?

A "yes" keeps Justice Carlos Muñiz for another six-year term; if "no" wins, the governor appoints a replacement. No Florida justice has lost a retention vote since the system began in 1976.

  • Background: University of Virginia and Yale Law; deputy attorney general and chief of staff to AG Pam Bondi; general counsel of the U.S. Education Department; Federalist Society member. DeSantis appointed him in January 2019. He was retained in 2020 with about 66% and served as chief justice from 2022 to 2026.

  • Notable votes: joined the 6–1 ruling in April 2024 that the state constitution's privacy clause does not protect abortion, which let the 6-week ban take effect. He was also in the 4–3 majority that put the 2024 abortion-rights amendment on the ballot, writing a concurrence, and joined the 5–2 decision that put the 2024 marijuana amendment on the ballot.

  • Evaluations: The Florida Bar stopped polling lawyers on retention in 2023. In its 2020 poll, 71% of responding lawyers who knew his work favored keeping him, tied for the lowest share among the judges rated that year.

  • No Sixth District Court of Appeal judges are on this year's retention ballot.

How to think about it: Retention asks whether a justice should keep the job, not who should replace him. Voters usually weigh judicial philosophy and specific rulings. Muñiz is one of the DeSantis appointees who now make up most of the court.

Circuit Judge, Ninth Circuit, Group 1 (nonpartisan runoff)

Temika Hampton-Johnson led the August primary with 44% to Charles Hart's 28%, ousting sitting Judge Mikaela Nix (27%). The winner serves a six-year term on the trial court for Orange and Osceola counties.

  • Temika Hampton-Johnson — 48. Stetson bachelor's and MBA, Florida A&M law degree (2013). Worked in the public defender's office in Tallahassee's 2nd Circuit, ran her own Orlando firm, worked as a state probation specialist, and served as an Army Reserve military police officer. Says personal values shouldn't drive rulings and supports remote hearings. Raised about $66,000, including about $45,000 of her own.

  • Charles Hart — UF law degree (2007). Former prosecutor and public defender in the 18th Circuit (Brevard and Seminole), private practice in Sanford, certified mediator. Chaired the Orange County Republican Party 2018–2022, is a Federalist Society member, lost a 2022 state House primary, and applied six times to judicial nominating commissions without advancing. Statewide constitutional amendments

The Legislature put all three amendments on the ballot, and each needs 60% of the vote to pass. Amendment 3, the property-tax overhaul, dwarfs the others: state economists estimate it would cut local, non-school revenue by $4.9 billion in its first year, rising to about $11.8 billion a year.

Amendment 1 — Budget Stabilization Fund

  • What it does: raises the cap on the state's rainy-day fund from 10% to 25% of general revenue and requires a deposit of $750 million a year (or whatever is needed to reach 25%) unless the fund is being tapped. Lawmakers could also withdraw money for "critical state needs" by two-thirds votes, within limits. Effective Jan. 5, 2027.

  • Yes means a larger, mandatory reserve. No keeps the 10% cap and emergency-only use.

  • Cost: House staff call the fiscal effect indeterminate but likely small. The fund holds about $5.1 billion today (9.75% of general revenue) and total reserves about $16.4 billion; in one TaxWatch scenario it would not reach 25% until 2049.

  • For: House Speaker Daniel Perez, the Republican Party of Florida and Florida TaxWatch, which says reserves prevent tax hikes or service cuts in downturns. David Jolly supports it with limits on how the money is used.

  • Against: Gov. DeSantis, who calls it foolish and wants surpluses used for tax cuts; Byron Donalds' campaign; several county Democratic parties, which say it locks away money for schools, health care and housing.

  • Origin: HJR 5019 (2025), passed 100–1 in the House and 29–4 in the Senate.

  • Read on it: An unusual split, with DeSantis and Donalds against and GOP legislative leaders for. The real question is whether the Constitution should force more saving, or leave surpluses for tax cuts or spending.

Amendment 2 — Property-tax exemption for farm equipment

  • What it does: exempts equipment and other movable property normally kept on agricultural land and used for farming or agritourism, if owned by the landowner or leaseholder. First applies in 2027.

  • Yes takes that property off local tax rolls. No keeps taxing it above the existing $25,000 business exemption.

  • Cost: local governments lose at least $28.7 million a year statewide (state estimate) or $31–34 million (TaxWatch); no state cost. An Orange County figure wasn't published.

  • For: sponsors Rep. Danny Alvarez and Sen. Keith Truenow, who say equipment taxed when bought shouldn't be taxed every year; Florida Farm Bureau; Florida Fruit & Vegetable Association; Florida Chamber; NFIB; Agriculture Commissioner Simpson. TaxWatch recommends yes but warns the cost shifts to other taxpayers, especially in small rural counties.

  • Against: Rep. Anna Eskamani, the Legislature's only "no" vote, who calls it too broad and tilted to big operations; Hillsborough Democrats, who prefer a capped break aimed at small farms.

  • Origin: HJR 1215 (2025), passed 110–1 in the House and 37–0 in the Senate.

  • Read on it: Small dollars statewide and little direct effect in urban Orange County. The debate is whether an uncapped break mostly helps large agricultural businesses.

Amendment 3 — Bigger homestead exemption, 5% cap, limits on local spending

  • What it does:

    • Raises the homestead exemption from county, city and special-district taxes to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, then indexes it to inflation. School taxes keep only today's $25,000 exemption.

    • Requires a uniform process letting counties and cities go further, up to a full exemption; special districts only by referendum.

    • People who weren't Florida residents on Dec. 31, 2026 keep the current exemption (about $50,000) until their fifth year, "to the extent permitted by the U.S. Constitution."

    • Cuts the yearly cap on assessment increases for non-homestead property (rentals, businesses, second homes) from 10% to 5%.

    • Limits county and city property-tax spending to public safety, education, infrastructure, natural resources, debt, employee retirement, and operations and administration, though local officials can approve other spending unless state law bars it.

    • Takes effect Jan. 1, 2027.

  • Yes means much lower non-school taxes for homestead owners. No keeps today's roughly $50,000 exemption and the 10% cap.

  • Official estimate (Revenue Estimating Conference, July 2026): statewide local revenue falls $4.93 billion in FY2027–28, rising to $11.83 billion a year by FY2031–32. For all Orange County local governments, $270 million the first year, rising to $781 million a year.

  • Local warnings: Orange County projects losing about $165 million in 2027 and $275 million in 2028; public safety takes 51% of its property-tax dollars. The City of Orlando projects $30 million in FY2028 and $50 million in FY2029, warns of slower 911 response, delayed road repairs, shorter park hours or new fees, and has floated a hiring freeze.

  • Savings for a City of Orlando homestead: Florida TaxWatch estimates about $1,113 in 2027 and $2,242 in 2028 for homes assessed above $250,000, if tax rates don't change. Homes assessed lower save less.

  • Renters and businesses: they get only the slower 5% cap. Nothing limits tax rates, so local governments could raise rates or fees on everyone else; critics call that a tax shift, while Florida Realtors argue the cap can help hold rents steady.

  • For: Gov. DeSantis (votes yes but says it fell short of what he wanted and isn't leading the campaign), Byron Donalds, CFO Blaise Ingoglia, Sen. Rick Scott, the state GOP, mayoral candidate Chris Messina, and Florida Realtors, the main funder of the Yes campaign at a reported $18 million. Their case: property taxes and local budgets have grown too fast, and homeowners need relief now.

  • Against: police and firefighter unions, the Florida League of Cities (85 of 411 cities couldn't keep public-safety funding at current levels), the Florida Association of Counties, Florida TaxWatch, the Florida Policy Institute, the Florida Democratic Party, and Republicans Ben Albritton (Senate President) and Kathleen Passidomo. TaxWatch cites the tax shift, the rushed process, no help for rural counties and the shaky residency rule, and prefers the 2027 Taxation and Budget Reform Commission. The Florida Sheriffs Association says it is significantly concerned about public-safety funding.

  • Legal fight: In August a Leon County judge ruled the Legislature's original title ("Save Our Homes From Excessive Property Taxes") and summary misleading; Attorney General Uthmeier rewrote them, and the rewrite is on your ballot. The five-year wait for new residents may draw lawsuits: the Florida Supreme Court struck down a nearly identical wait in 1983.

  • Origin: HJR 1-F, passed in a June 2026 special session 75–26 in the House and 30–9 in the Senate.

  • Polls: UNF (Oct. 12–19) 50% yes, 30% no; Mason-Dixon 55–26; JMI/Targoz 63–21; St. Pete Polls 42–35. Most polls show it short of 60%.

  • Read on it: A large, permanent shift of the local tax burden away from homestead owners. A homesteaded Mills 50 home assessed above $250,000 would likely save four figures a year by 2028. The trade-off is $30–50 million a year less for Orlando at first and hundreds of millions less for Orange County, while renters and businesses see little direct relief.

Orange County races

County Mayor (nonpartisan runoff)

Tiffany Moore Russell and Chris Messina are in a runoff to succeed term-limited Mayor Jerry Demings after finishing first and second in August. The race is officially nonpartisan, but Moore Russell is a Democrat and Messina a Republican. The mayor oversees a county budget of roughly $9 billion and chairs the County Commission.

August results: Moore Russell 32%, Messina 27%, Stephanie Murphy 26%, Mayra Uribe 15%. The three Democrats combined for about 73%; countywide, registered Democrats outnumber Republicans 38% to 28%, and a third of voters belong to neither party.

  • Tiffany Moore Russell — Orange County Clerk of Courts for more than 12 years until resigning Aug. 31 to run; before that, two terms on the County Commission, first elected in 2006 as its youngest member. FSU law degree.

    • Amendment 3: votes no, saying it would hurt county services and any tax cut needs a plan that doesn't push costs into fees.

    • Budget: as clerk, raised starting pay from $12 to about $17 an hour while cutting her budget; says she'd find efficiencies before any tax or fee increase.

    • Transit and growth: dedicated funding for a multimodal transit plan, including a transportation sales tax like the one voters rejected in 2022, plus night and weekend SunRail. Check road and stormwater capacity before approving development; protect rural and sensitive land.

    • Housing: public-private partnerships, preserving existing affordable units, mixed-income development.

    • Stadium: WFTV reported she was among the three primary candidates opposing the Orlando Dreamers' request for $975 million in tourist-tax money for an MLB stadium.

    • Support: Orlando YIMBY, among others. Raised about $189,000 by late May, trailing Murphy and Uribe at the time.

  • Chris Messina — CEO of a medical-marketing technology company; Harvard MBA and a master's in city planning; vice chair of the watchdog group Orange County Watch; never held office; calls himself a compassionate conservative. Finished second to Demings in 2022 with 21.5%.

    • Amendment 3: votes yes, the only one of the four primary candidates to back it; says growth would keep county revenue roughly level.

    • Budget: forensic audits and zero-based budgeting, aiming to cut $200 million a year without raising taxes. Opposes sales-tax increases as regressive and helped Orange County Watch fight one.

    • Transit and housing: rebuild LYNX as a technology-driven, point-to-point system; convert blighted strip malls into mixed-income housing.

    • Stadium: the only primary candidate to support the Dreamers' $975 million request, per WFTV.

    • Controversy: at a July debate he linked illegal immigration to higher housing costs, but Orlando Weekly said the paper he appeared to rely on attributed only a fraction of recent home-price growth to immigration. He also described Parramore with words like "junkies," drawing a rebuke from Moore Russell.

    • Money: says he raised less than his rivals; current totals not found.

What's at stake: The clearest local choice on taxes. Moore Russell would protect current services and pursue a transit tax; Messina would cut spending, reject new sales taxes and embrace Amendment 3's revenue cuts.

Clerk of Courts (fills the rest of the term, through 2028)

Roberta Walton Johnson (D) faces Terrell Thomas (NPA) to finish Moore Russell's term; she resigned early enough that voters, not the governor, choose her successor. The clerk keeps court records, collects fines and fees, and handles passports and marriage licenses; interim clerk Joyce Boudoin serves until then.

  • Roberta Walton Johnson (D) — 52, of Ocoee. Florida A&M social-work and law degrees; former legal-aid benefits attorney; aide to Moore Russell on the County Commission; ran the clerk's Self Help Center; now the clerk's general counsel. Lost County Commission races in 2014 and 2022. Won the August primary with 53% over former Property Appraiser Rick Singh.

    • Priorities: equal court access whether or not people can afford a lawyer; modern online services to cut waits; secure records.

    • Past case: Florida Politics reported that in 1996, at 20 and working as a bank teller, she cashed 32 stolen IRS refund checks for others in a larger scheme. She cooperated with federal agents and received five years' probation and an order to repay; she says she disclosed it to the Florida Bar and it doesn't define her.

    • Support: West Orange Political Alliance; won two straw polls. Raised about $65,000 by Oct. 2.

  • Terrell Thomas (NPA) — 48. About 20 years in the clerk's technology office (until 2018), then court-technology consulting, including for Tyler Technologies; founded a consulting firm in 2022. Has never held office and says he hasn't managed a multimillion-dollar budget.

    • Platform ("Clerk Forward"): staff development, mobile service stops, website and case-management upgrades, cybersecurity.

    • Raised about $27,000, including $16,000 of his own.

What's at stake: Walton Johnson brings legal and management experience inside the office; Thomas pitches technology modernization from outside it. The job is largely administrative.

Soil and Water Conservation Supervisor, District 5 (nonpartisan)

Zack Green and Jimm Middleton are running for an unpaid seat on a five-member board that advises landowners on conservation. A 2024 state-commissioned review found the district took in about $262 in fiscal 2023 and had no staff. Only District 5 residents, including Mills 50, vote in this race.

  • Jimm Middleton — says he has held leadership roles at Fortune 500 companies; priorities are outreach so landowners know what the district offers, and flood prevention. Ran for this seat in 2020.

  • Zack Green — did not answer the voter-guide questionnaire; background not found.

What's at stake: Very little money or power. Middleton is the only candidate who has publicly laid out priorities.

Local ballot question: school operating tax

The School Board is asking voters to renew its 1-mill operating property tax for four more years, July 2027 through June 2031. It has passed four times since 2010, most recently with 82% in 2022, and needs a simple majority. There are no City of Orlando or county charter questions on this ballot.

  • What it does: continues the current levy of $1 per $1,000 of taxable value for teacher and staff pay, academic programs, arts, athletics and student activities. Charter schools get their proportional share by law (about 8%), and the district must report spending every year.

  • Yes keeps the tax through June 2031. No ends it after June 2027.

  • Money: about $242 million this year, roughly 11% of the district's budget; the district projects about $255 million a year, funding around 2,000 positions. In 2024–25 it paid for academics and teacher retention ($103 million), arts ($67 million), staff pay ($29 million), charter schools ($19 million) and athletics ($9 million).

  • Cost to a homeowner: about $275 a year on a homestead assessed at $300,000, since school taxes keep only the $25,000 exemption (Amendment 3 doesn't change that). The district's own example is about $28 a month on a home appraised at $355,000. The rate stays the same, but bills rise as assessed values rise.

  • If it fails: the district warns of significant cuts, including positions; in 2022 it put the cost of failure at $177 million.

  • Context: the district expects about 3,000 fewer students in traditional schools, lost more than $23 million this year to falling enrollment, has closed or merged seven schools, and faces $145 million in added employee health costs over two years.

  • The debate: in May some board members wanted the ballot wording to stress teacher raises; the board kept the original language without guaranteed raises, citing uncertain revenue. School Board candidate Diana Moore criticizes that choice and points to $129 million in unassigned district funds.

  • For: Superintendent Maria Vazquez and board members including Chair Teresa Jacobs. Against: no organized opposition found; skeptics note that bills rise with values and raises aren't guaranteed.

Read on it: It is a renewal, not a new tax, and it has historically passed easily. A "no" would trim roughly 1 mill from your tax bill but remove about a ninth of the district's operating budget during an enrollment and budget squeeze.
