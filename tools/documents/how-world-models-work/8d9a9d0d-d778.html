How world models work, from the ground up

2026-10-30 ·

The big picture

A world model is a learned simulator: given the current state of an environment and an action, it predicts what happens next. An LLM predicts the next word; a world model predicts the next moment.

It is built from the same parts covered in How LLMs work, from the ground up: neural networks trained by gradient descent, usually transformers, learning from huge amounts of data. What changes is the data and the question. The data is video, sensor streams, and logged actions instead of text, and the question becomes "if I do this, what will I see next?"

That question is at the core of acting in the world. An agent that can answer it can try plans in imagination before committing to one. Engineers can use the same model as a simulator learned from recorded data instead of written from equations.

From LLMs to world models

Most of the machinery carries over unchanged. What differs is what goes in, what comes out, and one new input: the action.

The state-space view

In control-theory terms, a world model is a learned state-space model: a dynamics function and an observation function, both fit from data.

\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{z}_{t+1} &= f_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{a}_t) \\
\mathbf{o}_t &= g_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t)
\end{aligned}

Here z is an internal state, a is the action, o is the observation, and θ is the set of learned parameters. Nobody writes f or g by hand. Both are neural networks, trained until their predictions match what actually happened in the logged data.

Two things differ from a classical model. The state z is not a physical quantity like position or velocity; it is whatever vector training finds useful, and no one assigns its components meaning. And f is usually stochastic, predicting a distribution over next states, because the same action can lead to different outcomes (a pedestrian might or might not step out).

A short history

The idea is old. In 1943 the psychologist Kenneth Craik proposed that minds carry a small-scale model of reality for trying out alternatives, and model-based control and reinforcement learning have built on similar ideas for decades. A 2018 paper by David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber, titled simply "World Models," trained an agent inside its own learned dream of a video game and popularized the term in deep learning.

The phrase also gets used loosely for any internal picture of the world, as in debates over whether LLMs have one. This document means the explicit kind: a model that takes actions and predicts their consequences.

Anatomy of a world model

Most world models have four parts: an encoder, a latent state, a dynamics model, and a decoder. Many add small heads that predict reward or other quantities an agent cares about.

The encoder turns each observation into a latent state; the dynamics model advances that state one step per action, and feeding its output back in produces an imagined trajectory.

  • Encoder. Compresses each raw observation, such as a camera frame, into a compact vector or a small grid of tokens. It does the job a state estimator like a Kalman filter does in classical estimation: turning noisy, high-dimensional measurements into a state estimate. Many encoders also fold in recent history, because one frame can't show velocity.

  • Latent state. The model's internal summary of the world at one moment. It must keep whatever matters for predicting the future, such as where things are and how they're moving, and can drop the rest, such as the exact texture of the pavement.

  • Dynamics model. The core: given the latent state and an action, predict the next latent state. It is usually a transformer, a recurrent network, or a diffusion model, and it is where the model's "physics" lives.

  • Decoder. Turns a latent state back into an observation, such as a predicted video frame. It's needed to show a person what the model imagines or to train against pixels, but some designs skip it entirely.

  • Task heads (optional). Small networks that read the latent state and predict reward, collision risk, or whether an episode ends. A planning agent needs these to judge which imagined futures are good.

Running the dynamics model repeatedly, feeding each prediction back in as the next input, is called a rollout, or imagination. Rollouts in a compact latent space are cheap, so a model can imagine many futures in parallel. Pixel-level video models are far more expensive, and some run slower than real time.

Representing the world

Raw video is enormous, so world models almost never predict pixels directly. One 1080p frame holds about 6 million numbers (1,920 × 1,080 × 3 color channels), and a camera produces 30 or more frames per second. The first job is compression.

Learned compression

A video tokenizer is an encoder-decoder pair trained to squeeze video down and rebuild it. The encoder maps a short clip to a much smaller grid of latents, the decoder reconstructs the clip from them, and training minimizes the reconstruction error. After that, the world model works entirely on the small grid; the decoder is only used to show results.

NVIDIA's Cosmos Tokenizer is a typical example. It shrinks height and width by 8 or 16 times each and time by 4 or 8 times, for a total reduction of up to 2,048 times (8 × 16 × 16).

Tokenizers come in two flavors:

  • Continuous latents. Each grid position holds a short vector of real numbers. These suit diffusion models, described below.

  • Discrete tokens. Each position holds an integer from a fixed codebook, like a word ID in an LLM. These suit LLM-style next-token prediction with cross-entropy loss. The encoder's output is snapped to the nearest codebook entry, a step called quantization.

Patches

A transformer then cuts the latent grid into small spacetime patches, for example 2 × 2 positions in a single time step, and treats each patch as one token. Sequences get long: NVIDIA's Cosmos autoregressive model works with up to 121 frames at a time, about 50,000 tokens.

Other sensors

The same approach extends beyond cameras. Lidar point clouds are often converted to a bird's-eye-view grid; robot joint angles and vehicle states are short vectors embedded directly; multi-camera rigs are encoded per camera and fused. Some models predict 3D occupancy, meaning which cells of space are filled, which drops appearance and keeps geometry.

Three ways to predict the future

World models differ most in how the dynamics model makes its prediction. Three approaches dominate, and many current systems blend them.

1. Autoregressive tokens

Turn video into discrete tokens, then predict the next tokens exactly as an LLM predicts the next word, conditioned on past tokens and the action. Training uses cross-entropy loss, and everything in the LLM document carries over.

DeepMind's Genie (2024) is an example: an 11-billion-parameter model built from a video tokenizer, an autoregressive dynamics model, and a latent action model. The approach inherits the transformer's scaling behavior, but converting continuous video into discrete tokens loses fine detail.

2. Diffusion

A diffusion model learns to remove noise. In training, a real next frame is corrupted with random noise, and the network learns to recover the noise that was added, given the noisy frame, the noise level, the past frames, and the action:

L = \mathbb{E}\left[ \left\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{t+1},\, k,\, \mathbf{x}_{\le t},\, \mathbf{a}_t\right) \right\rVert^2 \right]

Here x⁽ᵏ⁾ is the next frame with noise level k mixed in and ε is the noise. To generate a frame, the model starts from pure noise and removes it over a series of passes, from a handful to dozens.

Diffusion produces the sharpest, most realistic images, which is why it dominates video generation. Google's GameNGen (2024) is a diffusion model that runs the game DOOM at over 20 frames per second on one TPU, predicting each frame from past frames and the player's inputs. Human raters were only slightly better than chance at telling short clips of it from the real game.

The costs are compute, since every frame needs several passes, and drift, since generated frames are fed back in as inputs. GameNGen countered drift by adding noise to its own past-frame inputs during training, so the model learned to correct imperfect inputs.

3. Latent prediction

The third approach skips pixels entirely: predict the next latent state and never reconstruct the image. The argument, championed by Yann LeCun, is that most pixel detail (leaves, reflections, texture) is unpredictable and irrelevant to acting, so modeling it wastes capacity.

  • Joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA). An encoder embeds video, and a predictor learns to predict the embeddings of hidden or future parts from the visible parts. Meta's V-JEPA 2 (2025) pretrained this way on over a million hours of internet video, with no action labels.

  • Recurrent state-space models (Dreamer). A recurrent latent state is updated each step from the previous state, the action, and the new observation; a decoder serves only as a training signal. DreamerV3 was the first algorithm to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch without human data, learning its behavior from imagined rollouts in its own world model.

Latent prediction has a known failure mode called collapse: if a model only has to predict its own embeddings, the trivial solution is to embed everything as the same constant. JEPA-style methods prevent this by computing targets with a slowly updated copy of the encoder.

The lines are blurring. Many current video systems run diffusion inside a transformer over latent patches, and latent planners often borrow encoders pretrained by generative models.

Where actions come from

A world model is only useful for decisions if it knows what each action does. That takes data pairing observations with the actions taken, and such data is scarce compared with ordinary video.

Action-conditioned training

The direct approach logs actions alongside sensor data: steering and throttle from a car, joint commands from a robot, button presses from a game. Each action is embedded as a vector and fed to the dynamics model at every step, usually added to the latent tokens or injected through attention.

Logged actions carry a subtle bias. A human driver brakes because they saw something, so a model can learn to associate braking with hazards ahead instead of learning what braking does. Models trained this way answer "what usually happens" better than "what would happen if I acted differently."

Pretrain on video, then add a little action data

Internet video is plentiful but has no action labels. A common recipe is to pretrain on huge amounts of unlabeled video to learn how the world looks and moves, then train a small action-conditioned model on top with a little labeled data.

V-JEPA 2 is the clearest example. After action-free pretraining, Meta trained an action-conditioned predictor, V-JEPA 2-AC, on under 62 hours of robot video from the DROID dataset. Deployed zero-shot on Franka robot arms in two different labs, it picked and placed objects by planning toward goal images.

Latent actions

Genie went further and learned actions from video with no labels at all. A separate latent action model looks at consecutive frames and must squeeze "what changed" into one of a few discrete codes, eight in Genie's case, which a decoder then uses to reconstruct the next frame. The codes come to mean things like "move left" or "jump," and a person can play the generated world using them as controls.

Genie learned this from about 30,000 hours of 2D platformer gameplay video. Its learned actions were consistent enough to infer what a player was doing in videos it had never seen, which opens the door to learning from the vast amount of unlabeled video online.

Using a world model

A trained world model gets used in four main ways: to plan, to train a policy in imagination, to generate training data, and to test a system before it touches the real world.

Planning: model predictive control with a learned model

A world model slots directly into a model predictive control (MPC) loop, with learned dynamics in place of hand-derived equations:

The world model sits inside the loop as the predictor, and replanning after every real step keeps its errors from compounding.

  1. Encode the current observation into a latent state.

  2. Propose many candidate action sequences over a short horizon.

  3. Roll each one out in the world model and score the predicted outcome, for example by distance to a goal or with a learned reward head.

  4. Execute only the first action of the best sequence.

  5. Observe the real result and repeat.

A common optimizer for step 2 is the cross-entropy method: sample candidate sequences from a distribution, keep the best few, refit the distribution to them, and repeat. V-JEPA 2-AC plans in an MPC loop like this, scoring candidates by how close the predicted embedding gets to the embedding of a goal image.

Replanning every step does what it does in classical MPC: it absorbs model error, because the plan is corrected against reality before errors compound.

Training a policy in imagination

Instead of planning online, an agent can learn a policy from imagined experience. Dreamer alternates between collecting real experience, refitting its world model, and training an actor-critic policy on imagined trajectories. Imagined experience is cheap, so the agent needs far fewer real interactions.

Generating training data

A generative world model can produce realistic synthetic sensor data, especially for events that are rare, dangerous, or expensive to record. Serious crashes are so rare per mile that even a large fleet records few of them; a world model can generate many variations of each one.

Testing in closed loop

The most consequential use for autonomy is closed-loop testing. The system under test drives inside the world model, and the world model responds to what it does. That differs from replaying a recorded drive, where the scene can't react if the vehicle deviates from what the original driver did.

Waymo uses its world model this way, simulating counterfactuals such as whether its driver could have proceeded more confidently instead of yielding. It contrasts this with reconstruction methods like 3D Gaussian splatting, whose images break down when the simulated route strays too far from the recorded one.

World models versus physics simulators

A physics simulator computes the future from equations someone wrote down; a world model predicts it from patterns in data. Each is strong where the other is weak.

DimensionPhysics-based simulatorLearned world model
Source of truthEquations, parameters, and hand-built assetsRecorded data
Physical accuracyHigh where the model is valid, and checkableApproximate; objects can vanish or ignore gravity
Sensor realismLimited by assets and rendering (the sim-to-real gap)Often photorealistic, since it learned from real sensors
Other agents' behaviorScripted or rule-basedLearned from real behavior, including messy cases
Rare scenariosAuthored one at a timeGenerated from a prompt, if training data covered something similar
RepeatabilityDeterministic for a given seedStochastic; the same input can give different outputs

The physics column is not hypothetical. NVIDIA tested its Cosmos world models against controlled gravity, collision, torque, and inertia scenarios built in its PhysX engine, and reported objects vanishing or appearing unexpectedly and motion that violated gravity.

Hybrids

In practice the two are converging, and the strongest systems combine them:

  • Neural rendering on top of physics. Run vehicle dynamics and traffic in a conventional simulator, then render sensor data with learned scene representations, such as neural radiance fields or 3D Gaussian splats reconstructed from real drives. Physics stays trustworthy while the images look real.

  • Simulation-to-real translation. Render a scene coarsely in a physics engine, then have a generative model repaint it photorealistically while keeping its structure. NVIDIA's Cosmos-Transfer1 does this, conditioning on segmentation, depth, and edge maps exported from a simulator.

  • Learned residuals. Keep the physics model and train a network to predict only its error against logged data, such as an unmodeled tire or aerodynamic effect.

  • Structure-conditioned world models. Feed a world model explicit structure, such as road layouts, object boxes, or signal states, so scenes stay controllable. Waymo's scene layout control works this way.

The split that's emerging: physics where correctness matters and modeling is tractable, learned models where realism matters and hand-modeling is hopeless, such as sensor appearance and human behavior.

Open problems

World models are improving fast, but several problems remain unsolved, and most matter more for engineering use than for demos.

  • Drift. Every prediction feeds the next, so small errors accumulate. DeepMind's own Genie 4 limitations note that visual details and object permanence shift as a simulation runs longer, and sessions last a few minutes rather than hours.

  • Physical correctness. Models learn what usually looks right, not the governing laws. A model can be photorealistic and still wrong about friction, mass, or stopping distance, and NVIDIA's Cosmos tests found objects vanishing and motion violating gravity.

  • Validation. For safety-critical use, this is the hardest problem: how do you show a learned simulator is accurate enough to certify a system? There is no equation to check, so the evidence must come from statistical comparison with real-world outcomes. Wayve reports that GAIA-3 test results closely mirror real-world driving, and it is working with the University of Warwick on DriveSafeSim, a UK government-funded project to validate world models for safety evaluation.

  • Many interacting agents. Modeling many independent agents reacting to one another is still hard; DeepMind lists it as a limitation of both Genie 3 and Genie 4.

  • Cause versus correlation. Trained on logged behavior, models can confuse what accompanies an action with what the action causes, as in the braking example above. Trustworthy "what if" simulation requires the model to have learned the cause.

  • Action data. Video is abundant; video paired with actions is scarce. Latent actions and pretrain-then-fine-tune recipes help, but robot interaction data is still a bottleneck.

  • Compute. Pixel-level models are expensive to run. Real-time generation is a recent achievement, and long, high-resolution, multi-sensor rollouts remain costly; Waymo built a separate, more efficient variant of its model to make long simulations affordable.

Where this meets simulation engineering

World models don't make physics-based simulation obsolete; they change where it adds the most value. These areas are shifting fastest:

  • Sensor simulation. This is where learned models are strongest and change is quickest. Rendering realistic camera and lidar data from hand-built assets is costly and never quite real, so Waymo and Wayve now generate sensor data with learned models, and Cosmos-Transfer1 repaints simulator output to look real. Physics-based sensor models still matter wherever results must be exact: geometry, timing, noise characteristics, and failure modes.

  • Scenario generation. Authoring edge cases one at a time gives way to prompting for them and sampling variations. The engineering work shifts to defining coverage: which scenarios matter, how to parameterize them, and how to know when testing is enough.

  • Validating the simulator itself. Someone has to establish how far a learned simulator can be trusted, for which scenarios, and with what error bounds. That is classic verification and validation, applied to a new kind of model.

  • Hybrid architectures. Many systems will keep physics at the core for vehicle dynamics and guidance, navigation, and control, with learned parts for appearance, other agents' behavior, and residual errors. Designing the interfaces between them, and deciding which parts may be learned, takes both kinds of expertise.

  • Closed-loop test infrastructure. Running an autonomy stack against a reactive world model at scale raises familiar questions in a harder form: reproducibility, metrics, and regression tracking when the simulator itself is stochastic.

Skills that carry over directly include state-space modeling and estimation, model predictive control, Monte Carlo analysis, verification and validation, and sensor modeling. The newer additions are training and evaluating generative models and building data pipelines for large logged datasets.
