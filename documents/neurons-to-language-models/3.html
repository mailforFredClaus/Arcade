1 · Introduction

From Neurons to Language Models·3 of 29·7 min read

Objectives. After this chapter you can:

  • say what separates a learned program from a written one;

  • name the three ingredients of any learning system and find them in a new one;

  • explain why learned features displaced hand-designed ones;

  • place the main milestones from the perceptron to LLMs and say what unlocked each.

#1.1 Two ways to get a program

There are two ways to make a computer map inputs to outputs. You can write the mapping down, or you can specify a family of mappings and let data choose one.

For some tasks writing it down works. Sorting, parsing, and orbit propagation have compact, exact descriptions. For others it has never worked. Nobody has written a program that recognizes a handwritten 7, transcribes speech, or translates Japanese to English by enumerating rules, despite decades of serious attempts. The rules exist in some sense, because people perform these tasks, but people cannot articulate them.

Machine learning is the second route. You collect examples of the desired behavior, choose a flexible family of functions, and search that family for a member that reproduces the examples. If the search and the family are good, the function you find also works on inputs it has never seen. That last step, from seen to unseen, is called generalization, and it is the whole point.

If you have fit a model to measurements, you have done this already. Least-squares regression, system identification, and calibration are all instances. What is new in deep learning is scale and expressiveness: function families with 10^6 to 10^12 parameters, flexible enough to represent almost anything, fit to datasets too large for any person to read.

Checkpoint 1.1. A Kalman filter with fixed, hand-derived dynamics and noise covariances estimates a state from data. Is it a learned program in the sense above? What would you change to make it one?

#1.2 The recipe

Every system in this book follows one template. There is a dataset of N input-target pairs, a model f with parameters θ, and a per-example loss ℓ that scores a prediction against its target. Training means solving

θ⋆  =  arg⁡min⁡θ  L(θ),L(θ)  =  1N∑i=1Nℓ(fθ(xi), yi).\theta^\star \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta}\; L(\theta), \qquad L(\theta) \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(f_\theta(x_i),\, y_i\big).

The three ingredients:

IngredientQuestion it answersIn an LLM
ModelWhich functions can be represented?A Transformer mapping a token sequence to next-token probabilities
LossWhat counts as a good fit?Cross-entropy between predicted and actual next token
OptimizerHow is a good member of the family found?A variant of stochastic gradient descent

Figure 1.1: the three ingredients in motion. The model predicts, the loss scores the prediction, backpropagation turns the score into a gradient, and the optimizer nudges the weights. One trip round the loop is one training step.

The template looks innocent. Three hard questions hide inside it, and they organize the field.

  1. Representation. What family should f come from? Too small and nothing in it fits. Too unstructured and the search is hopeless. Chapters 4, 8, and 11 are about this.

  2. Optimization. The loss is a non-convex function of up to a trillion variables. Why should any local search find a good point? Chapters 5 through 7.

  3. Generalization. The objective measures fit on the training set. You care about inputs outside it. Why should one imply the other, especially when the model has more parameters than there are examples? Chapter 3 gives the classical answer and Chapter 20 explains why the classical answer is incomplete.

#1.3 Why "deep"

Before about 2012, the standard pipeline for perception had two stages. An engineer designed features: edge histograms for images, cepstral coefficients for audio, word counts for text. Then a simple learned model, often linear, mapped features to outputs. The learning was shallow. Most of the intelligence was in the feature design, and progress was limited by how fast people could invent features.

A deep network learns the features too. It is a composition of many simple parameterized layers, and all layers are trained together against the final objective. Early layers come to compute simple features of the input, later layers compute features of features. Nobody specifies what the intermediate features should be. They are whatever makes the final loss small.

This is called representation learning, and it is the central idea of the subject. It won for a practical reason: a method whose quality is bounded by human ingenuity improves slowly, while a method whose quality is bounded by data and computation improved by many orders of magnitude as those became cheap. Richard Sutton called this pattern "the bitter lesson": across the history of AI, general methods that exploit computation have eventually beaten methods that encode human knowledge of the domain.

Checkpoint 1.2. Stack two linear maps, y = W2 (W1 x). What family of functions can this represent? What does that say about the need for something other than linear maps between layers? (Chapter 4 answers this.)

Three things recur. First, the ideas usually preceded their success by decades: backpropagation, convolution, and recurrent networks all date from the 1980s. Second, what turned ideas into results was data and computation. Third, the list of algorithmic ideas that mattered is short: backpropagation, a few tricks that keep gradients healthy in deep networks, and attention. This book spends most of its pages on that short list.

#1.5 The destination, in one paragraph

Here is where the book is going. Each bolded term becomes precise in the chapter noted.

An LLM operates on tokens, integer codes for fragments of text (Chapter 9). Each token is mapped to a vector, its embedding. The sequence of vectors passes through a stack of identical Transformer blocks (Chapter 11), each of which lets every position gather information from earlier positions through attention and then transforms it with a small multilayer perceptron (Chapter 4). The final vector at each position is mapped to a score for every token in the vocabulary, and a softmax (Chapter 3) turns scores into probabilities for the next token. Training adjusts all parameters by backpropagation (Chapter 5) and stochastic gradient descent (Chapter 6) to raise the probability of the token that actually came next, across trillions of tokens of text (Chapter 13). To generate, the model samples a token from its predicted distribution, appends it, and repeats (Chapter 15). A chat assistant is this same loop after additional training on dialogue and on human preferences (Chapter 14), wrapped in software that supplies documents and tools (Chapter 16).

Nothing in that paragraph is hidden or simplified. The surprise of the last decade is that this is all there is, and the puzzle of Chapter 20 is why it is enough.

#1.6 Two errors to avoid

Two opposite mistakes are common, and both get in the way of understanding.

The first is to take fluent output as evidence of human-like cognition behind it. These systems fail in ways no person would, and succeed by routes no person uses. Their abilities have to be measured, not inferred from how they sound.

The second is to conclude from the simplicity of the training objective that the result must be shallow: "it just predicts the next word." The objective says what is optimized. It says little about what computation the optimized system performs. Predicting the next line of a proof well requires something that functions like following the proof. How far that goes is an empirical question, and Chapters 19 and 20 take it up.
