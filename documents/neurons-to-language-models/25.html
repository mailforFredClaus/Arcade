From Neurons to Language Models·25 of 29·19 min read

Objectives. After this chapter you can:

  • say in which senses these systems learn, and in which they do not;

  • explain why classical statistics fails to account for deep networks, and describe the partial explanations that exist;

  • state the equivalence of prediction and compression and what follows from it;

  • give the strongest version of both sides of the debate over whether language models understand;

  • list the questions that remain open.

The earlier chapters explained how these systems are built. This one asks why the construction works and what the result is. Much less is settled here. To keep the status of each claim clear, the text marks it: [Theorem] for mathematics, [Empirical] for regularities observed repeatedly, [Contested] for interpretation on which informed people disagree.

#20.1 Three questions

  1. Does it learn? In what sense of the word, and how does that compare with what people do?

  2. Why does the recipe work? Why does gradient descent on a huge non-convex function find good solutions, and why do those solutions hold up on new data?

  3. What has been learned? Is there anything in a trained language model that deserves to be called understanding?

#20.2 What "learning" means here

The standard definition is Tom Mitchell's (1997): a program learns from experience with respect to a task and a performance measure if its performance on the task, by that measure, improves with experience. By that definition the answer is yes, trivially: the loss goes down as data is consumed. But the word carries more, and it helps to separate three senses.

SenseMeaningStatus for deep networks
FittingParameters are adjusted so that outputs match the training dataYes, by construction
GeneralizingPerformance holds on new inputs drawn from the same sourceYes, measurably, and for reasons not fully understood
AbstractingThe underlying rule has been acquired, so performance holds on inputs unlike any seenSometimes. This is the real question

The same network can memorize or generalize. Lab 20B trains one architecture twice on handwritten digits: once with the true labels, once with labels replaced by random ones.

The network fits random labels perfectly. It has the capacity to memorize a thousand arbitrary associations, and it does so when the data offer nothing else. On real labels the same network with the same optimizer finds a solution that generalizes. Fitting and generalizing are different outcomes of one procedure, and which occurs depends on the data [Empirical] (Zhang and others, 2017). Hold on to this result. It destroys the simplest explanation of generalization, as the next section shows.

How it differs from human learning.

  • Sample efficiency. A person reads perhaps a billion words in a lifetime. A language model trains on ten thousand times that. Whatever the model does, it is far less efficient at extracting structure from each example.

  • Passivity. The model predicts text that already exists. It does not act, experiment, or choose what to observe during pretraining.

  • Two timescales, no bridge. Section 13.6 distinguished learning in the weights, which is slow and permanent, from learning in the context, which is fast and discarded. People consolidate short-term experience into long-term memory continuously. A deployed model, by default, does not: its weights are frozen and each conversation starts from the same state.

Is in-context learning really learning? Within a single context it satisfies Mitchell's definition: performance on a task improves with examples. It leaves no trace. Whether that counts is a matter of definition, and the definition matters less than keeping the two mechanisms distinct.

Nothing mysterious happens mechanically. Training is curve fitting by gradient descent in a space of up to 10^12 dimensions. The mystery is which curve gets fit.

#20.3 The generalization puzzle

Chapter 3 gave the classical account. A model family has a capacity. The gap between training and test error is bounded by something like capacity divided by the number of examples. Too much capacity and the model fits noise. Test error traces a U as capacity grows, and the art is to stop at the bottom.

Deep networks violate every part of this.

  1. They have enough capacity to fit random labels. So any bound that depends only on the model family's capacity must be vacuous for them: the same family that generalizes on real data is, provably, rich enough not to [Theorem, given the experiment].

  2. They are trained to zero training error, deep in the regime the U-curve calls overfitting, and they generalize anyway [Empirical].

  3. Past the point of exact fit, making the model larger often makes test error go down again [Empirical]. This is double descent (Belkin and others, 2019; Nakkiran and others, 2019).

Figure 20.1: error rises as the model approaches the size at which it can just fit all 100 training points, then falls again as the model grows far past that size. The largest model ends below the best small one.

Up to p = 50 this is the classical U: the test error bottoms out at 0.72 and then climbs. At p = 100, where the number of parameters equals the number of data points, it explodes. Then it falls, and keeps falling. With a hundred times more parameters than data points, the test error is 0.42, well below the classical optimum, against a noise floor of 0.25.

The third column explains it. At p = 100 there is exactly one set of coefficients that fits the data, and it must contort to pass through every noisy point: its norm is 20. For p greater than 100 there are infinitely many exact fits, and the method used, the pseudoinverse, returns the one of smallest norm. The more features there are, the smaller and smoother that minimum-norm solution can be.

The lesson generalizes beyond this toy: the number of parameters is not the right measure of complexity. What matters is which of the many solutions that fit the data the training procedure selects.

#20.4 Partial explanations

No complete theory of generalization in deep learning exists. Several partial accounts are each supported and each incomplete.

Implicit bias of the optimizer. Gradient descent does not return an arbitrary minimizer. For overparameterized least squares started from zero, it converges to the minimum-norm solution, the one the lab used [Theorem]. For logistic regression on separable data, it converges in direction to the maximum-margin separator, with no regularizer in the objective [Theorem] (Soudry and others, 2018). For deep networks the bias is less well characterized, but gradient descent is observed to fit simple, low-frequency structure first and to favor low-norm, low-rank solutions [Empirical]. The algorithm itself is a regularizer.

Noise and flat minima. Stochastic gradient descent with small batches is biased toward wide basins of the loss surface, because narrow ones cannot hold a noisy iterate. A solution in a wide basin is insensitive to perturbation of its weights, which means the weights can be specified with few bits. By the argument of Section 20.6, short descriptions generalize [Empirical, with the caveat of Section 6.7 that sharpness depends on parameterization].

A prior toward simple functions. If you sample the weights of a network at random and ask what function it computes, simple functions are overwhelmingly more probable than complex ones. The architecture induces a prior over functions that is heavily weighted toward simplicity before any training [Empirical] (Valle-Pérez and others, 2019).

The infinite-width limit. As a network's width goes to infinity, training by gradient descent becomes equivalent to regression with a fixed kernel, the neural tangent kernel [Theorem] (Jacot and others, 2018). In that limit both optimization and generalization can be analyzed exactly. But real networks do something the limit excludes: they change their internal features during training, and they outperform their own kernel approximations. The limit explains trainability better than it explains what is learned.

Lottery tickets. A large trained network contains a small subnetwork that, trained alone from the same initial values, matches the full network [Empirical] (Frankle and Carbin, 2019). On this view overparameterization helps the search: a larger network holds more candidate subnetworks, so one of them is more likely to start in a good position.

Compression bounds. Classical bounds fail because they measure the capacity of the whole family. Bounds that instead measure how far the particular trained network can be compressed, in the PAC-Bayes framework, give guarantees that are not vacuous [Theorem plus empirical] (Dziugaite and Roy, 2017). Networks that generalize turn out to be highly compressible. The solution found is simple even though the family is not.

The data. None of this would work on arbitrary data. Natural data have structure: images and sentences occupy a tiny, low-dimensional part of the space of all pixel arrays and token strings, and they are compositional. The priors of the architecture and the optimizer happen to suit that structure. Random labels have none, and are memorized. That learning works says something about the world's data as well as about the algorithm.

Synthesis. Generalization arises from three things together: structure in the data, a prior toward simplicity from the architecture, and a further bias toward simplicity from the optimizer. Each has been demonstrated. What is missing is a theory that predicts, in advance, how well a given network will generalize on given data.

Grokking: watching the two solutions compete. On small algorithmic tasks such as modular arithmetic, a network can reach perfect training accuracy while test accuracy sits at chance, and then, after training continues for ten or a hundred times longer, test accuracy jumps to perfect [Empirical] (Power and others, 2022). Mechanistic analysis shows what happens: a memorizing solution forms fast, a solution implementing the actual rule forms slowly underneath it, and weight decay eventually favors the rule because it achieves the same fit with smaller weights (Nanda and others, 2023). This is as clean a demonstration as exists that memorizing and learning the rule are distinct solutions, that one network can hold both, and that training pressure can move it from the first to the second.

#20.5 Why optimization succeeds

Section 6.7 listed what is observed. In brief: with enough parameters, poor local minima are rare, saddle points are escaped, and good solutions form connected regions. Residual connections and normalization shape the loss surface to be easier to descend. In the infinite-width limit, convergence to a global minimum is proved [Theorem]. For networks of practical size it is observed and not proved.

One idea ties optimization and generalization together. Overparameterization makes the set of solutions large. A large solution set is easy to find, which helps optimization. And within a large solution set there is room for the optimizer's preferences to pick a simple member, which helps generalization. Extra parameters are slack that the training procedure spends on simplicity.

#20.6 Prediction is compression

There is an exact equivalence between predicting data and compressing it [Theorem] (Shannon, 1948).

Given a model that assigns probability p to a sequence, arithmetic coding encodes that sequence in about −log₂ p bits. Conversely, any compression scheme implicitly assigns probabilities. So the cross-entropy loss of a language model, measured in bits, is the number of bits per token it would need to encode the text. Minimizing the training loss is minimizing compressed size.

Your 800,000-parameter model predicts held-out text better than general-purpose compressors. The comparison is of predictive quality only: a fair accounting of compression would also charge for transmitting the model's weights, which here are larger than the text. Large language models, used as compressors, far exceed standard tools on text, and through in-context adaptation do surprisingly well even on images and audio they were never trained on (Delétang and others, 2023).

Why this matters for understanding. To compress data you must find its regularities, since only regular data can be described briefly. The minimum description length principle makes this a theory of learning: the best hypothesis is the one minimizing the length of the hypothesis plus the length of the data encoded with its help. Solomonoff's theory of induction (1964) gives the idealized form: predict by weighting every program that reproduces the data, with shorter programs weighted exponentially more. It is uncomputable, but it identifies learning with the search for short programs that generate what has been observed.

Seen this way, pretraining is a search, by gradient descent, for a compact program that generates human text. Spelling is a regularity, so the program captures it. Grammar is a deeper one. Facts about the world are regularities in what people write. So are patterns of valid inference.

The argument has a strong form and a weak form. [Contested] The strong form says that text is produced by people reasoning about a world, so the shortest program that predicts text well must model that world and that reasoning. The weak form observes that gradient descent finds a short program, not the shortest, and that a patchwork of shallow statistical shortcuts might compress nearly as well in practice. The disagreement of Section 20.7 is largely a disagreement about which form describes actual models.

A popular image treats an LLM as lossy compression, "a blurry JPEG of the web" (Chiang, 2023), with hallucinations as compression artifacts. The image captures something true about recall of specific facts. It misses that a model can combine what it has stored to answer questions that appear nowhere in its training data, which a decompressed image cannot do.

#20.7 Does it understand?

This section presents two positions at their strongest and then tries to say where the evidence stands.

The deflationary position.

  • Form is not meaning. Bender and Koller (2020) argue that meaning is a relation between linguistic form and something outside language: the world, or a speaker's communicative intent. A system exposed only to form has no access to the other end of that relation and so cannot learn meaning, however much form it sees.

  • Syntax is not semantics. Searle's Chinese Room (1980): a person who follows rules for manipulating Chinese symbols, producing correct replies without knowing Chinese, does not understand Chinese. A program is rule-following symbol manipulation. So running a program, by itself, is not understanding.

  • The grounding problem. Symbols defined only in terms of other symbols never touch what they are about (Harnad, 1990).

  • The behavioral evidence. Models fail in ways that a system that understood would not: they contradict themselves, assert falsehoods fluently, break under rephrasing, and stumble on altered versions of familiar problems. On this view their successes reflect an immense store of patterns, and the phrase "stochastic parrots" (Bender, Gebru, and others, 2021) expresses it.

The functional position.

  • Understanding is as understanding does. The only tests we have for understanding in anyone are behavioral and, lately, mechanistic. A system that uses concepts correctly in new situations, draws the right inferences, and explains itself has the capacities the word names.

  • The internals are not parrot-like. Chapter 19 described models that build representations of board state from move sequences alone, of spatial and temporal relations, and that implement real algorithms, such as trigonometric addition, where a lookup table would have fit the training data. These are structured models of the domain, found because they predict well.

  • Form carries more than expected. The Othello result is a direct counterexample to the claim that nothing about the world behind a sequence can be recovered from the sequence. The statistical structure of language mirrors a great deal of the relational structure of what it describes.

  • Grounding is a matter of degree and is increasing. Models now take images and audio as input and act through tools. And much human knowledge is itself acquired from language, not from direct contact with its subject.

  • Replies to Searle. The person in the room does not understand, but the person is only a component. The question is whether the whole system does. Searle rejects this reply; the argument has continued for forty years without resolution.

Three clarifications that help.

  1. "Understanding" is not one thing. It bundles correct use, inference, explanation, transfer to new cases, grounding in perception and action, and perhaps conscious grasp. Current models have some of these to a high degree and others weakly or not at all. A yes-or-no question over the bundle has no determinate answer.

  2. Separate three questions. What can the system do? That is measurable (Chapter 17). How does it do it? That is the subject of interpretability (Chapter 19). Is there any experience accompanying the processing? No current evidence bears on that either way, and it should not be smuggled into the first two.

  3. The positions make different predictions. If models rely on surface statistics, performance should collapse on problems that are far from the training distribution but structurally simple, and internals should look like lookup. If models build abstractions, performance should transfer and internals should contain compact algorithms. Both patterns are observed, in different tasks and at different scales.

Where the evidence stands. [Contested, but this much is supported]

  • Language models learn more than surface co-occurrence. They form structured internal representations of the domains they model and use them in computing outputs.

  • Their competence is uneven in a way no human's is. Fluency far outruns reliability. They often use a shallow heuristic where one suffices, and it is hard to tell from outside when that is happening.

  • They lack reliable knowledge of their own knowledge, and their explanations of their own outputs are not dependable reports of their computation (Section 16.3).

"It is only autocomplete" understates what has been found inside these systems. "It understands as a person does" claims more than any evidence shows. The accurate statement is longer and less satisfying than either.

One fact belongs on the table: the text of this book was drafted by a large language model at a reader's request. That does not settle anything in this section, because the question is about mechanism and not about output. It is a data point all the same.

#20.8 Why did scale work?

Sutton's bitter lesson (Chapter 1) is an observation, not an explanation. Four considerations make the success of scaling less surprising in retrospect.

  • The objective is universal. Any task expressible in text is a special case of predicting text (Section 9.3).

  • The data contain the products of thought. Human text records explanations, arguments, derivations, and corrections. A predictor of that text is pushed toward whatever produces them.

  • Improvement is smooth. Loss falls as a power law in scale with no plateau in sight (Section 13.4), so investment was rewarded predictably at each step.

  • The architecture is general. A Transformer is a differentiable computer with few built-in assumptions (Section 8.4). What it can represent grows with its size, and gradient descent can find programs in it.

The counter-considerations. [Contested] The exponent of that power law is small, so each improvement costs a multiple of the last. The sample inefficiency relative to people is enormous and unexplained. Capabilities that people have, such as learning continually from experience, long-horizon planning, and acting in the physical world, are weak or bolted on. Some researchers conclude that scaling the present recipe will suffice for whatever is still missing. Others conclude that new ideas are required. Nobody knows, and confident forecasts in either direction have a poor record.

#20.9 Open questions

  1. What theory predicts the generalization of a specific network on specific data before it is trained?

  2. Among the many algorithms that fit the training data, which does gradient descent find, and why that one?

  3. What are the limits of in-context learning, and what determines them?

  4. When does a model's internal representation amount to a model of the world, and how can that be established from the weights?

  5. Can a model's actual reasons for an output be made reliably accessible, to others and to the model itself?

  6. Is the gap in sample efficiency between these systems and people a matter of scale, of architecture, of objective, or of something not yet conceived?

  7. Do systems of this kind have experiences or interests that matter morally? There is no consensus, no accepted test, and a growing view that the question deserves serious study and should not be dismissed in either direction.

A reader who has worked through this book can read the primary literature on every one of these.

#Lab 20: two experiments on generalization

20A. Double descent.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d, n_train, n_test, noise = 20, 100, 2000, 0.5
w_true = rng.normal(size=d) / np.sqrt(d)
def make(n):
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, d))
    return X, X @ w_true + noise * rng.normal(size=n)
Xtr, ytr = make(n_train); Xte, yte = make(n_test)

def features(X, W):                       # random ReLU features: a one-hidden-layer network
    return np.maximum(X @ W, 0)           # whose first layer is frozen at its random initialization

print("features  train MSE  test MSE   ||coef||")
for p in [5, 20, 50, 80, 95, 100, 105, 120, 200, 500, 2000, 10000]:
    tr, te, nm = [], [], []
    for trial in range(20):
        W = rng.normal(size=(d, p)) / np.sqrt(d)
        Ftr, Fte = features(Xtr, W), features(Xte, W)
        coef = np.linalg.pinv(Ftr) @ ytr              # least squares; minimum-norm solution when p > n
        tr.append(np.mean((Ftr @ coef - ytr) ** 2)); te.append(np.mean((Fte @ coef - yte) ** 2))
        nm.append(np.linalg.norm(coef))
    print(f"{p:8d}  {np.mean(tr):9.4f}  {np.median(te):8.3f}  {np.median(nm):9.2f}")
print(f"test MSE of predicting zero: {np.mean(yte ** 2):.3f};  noise floor: {noise ** 2:.3f}")

The output is the table in Section 20.3. Test errors are medians over 20 random feature sets.

20B. Fitting random labels.

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits

torch.manual_seed(0)
digits = load_digits()
X = torch.tensor(digits.data, dtype=torch.float32) / 16.0
y = torch.tensor(digits.target)
perm = torch.randperm(len(X)); tr, te = perm[:1000], perm[1000:]
y_random = y.clone(); y_random[tr] = torch.randint(0, 10, (1000,))     # destroy the labels' meaning

for name, labels in [("true labels", y), ("random labels", y_random)]:
    torch.manual_seed(1)
    net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 512), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(512, 512), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(512, 10))
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
    for step in range(3000):
        idx = tr[torch.randint(0, 1000, (100,))]
        loss = F.cross_entropy(net(X[idx]), labels[idx])
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    with torch.no_grad():
        acc_tr = (net(X[tr]).argmax(1) == labels[tr]).float().mean().item()
        acc_te = (net(X[te]).argmax(1) == y[te]).float().mean().item()
    print(f"{name:14s} train accuracy {acc_tr:.3f}   test accuracy (true labels) {acc_te:.3f}")
