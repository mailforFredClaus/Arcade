Each chapter ends with its own sources. This appendix gives a shorter path: the books worth owning, lecture series that pair well with this text, and the primary papers in an order that tells the story. Everything is cited by author and title, which is enough to find it. The citations were written from memory and not checked against the sources, so confirm details such as years before quoting them.

#Twenty-four papers, in order

Read in this order, the papers retrace the book. For each, read the abstract, the figures, and the method section first. You now have the background for all of them.

Foundations

  1. Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors" (1986).

  2. LeCun, Bottou, Bengio, and Haffner, "Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition" (1998).

  3. Bengio, Ducharme, Vincent, and Jauvin, "A neural probabilistic language model" (2003).

  4. Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton, "ImageNet classification with deep convolutional neural networks" (2012).

Making deep networks work

  1. Kingma and Ba, "Adam: a method for stochastic optimization" (2014).

  2. Ioffe and Szegedy, "Batch normalization" (2015).

  3. He, Zhang, Ren, and Sun, "Deep residual learning for image recognition" (2015).

Language and attention

  1. Mikolov and others, "Distributed representations of words and phrases and their compositionality" (2013).

  2. Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio, "Neural machine translation by jointly learning to align and translate" (2014).

  3. Vaswani and others, "Attention is all you need" (2017).

Large language models

  1. Devlin, Chang, Lee, and Toutanova, "BERT" (2018).

  2. Radford and others, "Language models are unsupervised multitask learners" (2019).

  3. Brown and others, "Language models are few-shot learners" (2020).

  4. Kaplan and others, "Scaling laws for neural language models" (2020).

  5. Hoffmann and others, "Training compute-optimal large language models" (2022).

  6. Ouyang and others, "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback" (2022).

  7. Hu and others, "LoRA: low-rank adaptation of large language models" (2021).

  8. Wei and others, "Chain-of-thought prompting elicits reasoning in large language models" (2022).

Beyond text

  1. Ho, Jain, and Abbeel, "Denoising diffusion probabilistic models" (2020).

  2. Radford and others, "Learning transferable visual models from natural language supervision" (2021).

Understanding what was built

  1. Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht, and Vinyals, "Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization" (2017).

  2. Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal, "Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias-variance trade-off" (2019).

  3. Elhage and others, "A mathematical framework for Transformer circuits" (2021).

  4. Bricken and others, "Towards monosemanticity: decomposing language models with dictionary learning" (2023).

#Keeping current

This book stops at fundamentals on purpose. The field's results appear first as preprints, and specifics go stale within months. Three habits help.

  • Read primary sources. For a new model, read its technical report or system card, not coverage of it.

  • Ask of every claim the questions of Chapter 17: measured on what, compared with what, with what uncertainty, and could the test data have been seen in training?

  • Place each new result in the template of Chapter 1. Is it a change to the model, the loss, the optimizer, the data, or the system around the model? Most are refinements of one of these, and the template tells you how much else must change with it.
