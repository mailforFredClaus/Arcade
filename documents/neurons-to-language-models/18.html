13 · Pretraining and scale

From Neurons to Language Models·18 of 29·10 min read

Objectives. After this chapter you can:

  • explain the pretrain-then-adapt paradigm and why next-token prediction is a rich objective;

  • state the empirical scaling laws and the compute-optimal trade between model size and data;

  • estimate the parameters, training computation, and memory of a model from its configuration;

  • describe the main engineering techniques that make large runs possible;

  • explain in-context learning and the debate about emergent abilities.

#13.1 A change of paradigm

Until about 2018, each language task had its own model, trained on its own labeled dataset: one for sentiment, one for translation, one for question answering. Labeled data was the bottleneck.

The replacement has two phases. Pretraining fits a large model to a very large corpus of unlabeled text with a self-supervised objective. Adaptation then specializes that model with comparatively little task-specific effort. GPT and BERT (2018) adapted by fine-tuning on labeled examples. GPT-3 (2020) showed that a large enough model needs no fine-tuning for many tasks: describing the task in the prompt, perhaps with a few examples, is enough.

Figure 13.1: the three stages a deployed model passes through. This chapter covers the first box. Chapters 14 to 16 cover the other two.

A model pretrained this way and reused across tasks is called a foundation model. The economic logic is that one very expensive training run is amortized over an unlimited number of uses.

#13.2 Why predicting the next token teaches so much

The pretraining objective is the cross-entropy of Section 9.3, nothing more. Consider what lowers that loss on real text.

To predict the continuation ofIt helps to have learned
"The keys to the cabinet"Syntax: the verb agrees with "keys", not "cabinet"
"The capital of Australia is"A fact
"She put the ice cream in the oven, so it"How the physical world behaves
"If x + 3 = 7 then x ="Arithmetic
A function body, given its name and signatureWhat the code is supposed to do
A reply in a dialogueWhat the other speaker knows and wants
The last line of a proofThe proof

Every regularity in how text is produced is a source of predictability, and gradient descent pursues any available reduction in loss. Syntax pays off on every sentence and is learned first. Facts pay off rarely each but there are many of them. Reasoning pays off wherever text records the results of reasoning.

In the limit, predicting text perfectly would require modeling everything about the process that generated it, including the knowledge and intentions of the writers. No model reaches that limit. But the objective is not shallow, and it does not run out. This is the answer to the objection that the model "only" predicts the next word.

#13.3 Data

Pretraining corpora combine web pages, books, scientific papers, code repositories, and other sources, to a total of 10^12 to 10^13 tokens or more. Raw collection is the easy part. The pipeline that follows matters as much as the volume:

  • Deduplication, exact and near-exact. The web is highly repetitive, and duplicates waste computation and encourage memorization.

  • Filtering for quality, language, and unwanted content, often with classifiers that are themselves small models.

  • Mixing: deciding the proportions of sources, since the model becomes what it reads.

Increasingly, training sets also include synthetic data generated by other models, for example worked solutions to problems. The provenance, licensing, and consent questions around training data are contested legally and ethically, and are not settled.

#13.4 Scaling laws

The central empirical discovery behind modern LLMs is that performance improves smoothly and predictably with scale.

Power laws. Kaplan and others (2020) trained Transformer language models spanning many orders of magnitude and found that held-out loss falls as a power law in each of three resources, when the other two are not limiting:

L(N)  ∝  N−αN,L(D)  ∝  D−αD,L(C)  ∝  C−αC,L(N) \;\propto\; N^{-\alpha_N}, \qquad L(D) \;\propto\; D^{-\alpha_D}, \qquad L(C) \;\propto\; C^{-\alpha_C},

where N is the number of non-embedding parameters, D the number of training tokens, and C the training computation. The exponents are small: about 0.076 for parameters in that study. On a log-log plot the points fall on a straight line with no visible plateau.

Two features of this result shaped the field. The trend is predictable, so one can forecast what a tenfold larger run will achieve before paying for it. And it is insensitive to architectural detail within the Transformer family: shape, depth-to-width ratio, and similar choices matter far less than total size.

The small exponent cuts both ways. A power of 0.076 means a fourfold larger model for each 10 percent reduction in loss (Problem 13.4). Progress by scale is reliable and expensive.

The compute-optimal trade. Training computation is roughly proportional to the product of model size and data. Given a fixed budget, should you train a larger model on less data or a smaller one on more? Hoffmann and others (2022), the "Chinchilla" paper, fit a joint law of the form

L(N,D)  =  E  +  ANα  +  BDβ,L(N, D) \;=\; E \;+\; \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} \;+\; \frac{B}{D^{\beta}},

with reported exponents near 0.34 and 0.28. E is the irreducible loss, the entropy of text itself. The second term is error from finite capacity; the third, error from finite data. Minimizing at fixed computation gives a simple rule: scale parameters and tokens in equal proportion, at about 20 tokens per parameter.

By that rule, earlier large models were oversized for their data. GPT-3 had 175 billion parameters and saw about 300 billion tokens, under 2 tokens per parameter. The Chinchilla model, with 70 billion parameters trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, outperformed a 280-billion-parameter model trained with the same computation.

The 20-to-1 ratio minimizes training cost for a given quality. Deployment shifts the balance. A model is trained once and run billions of times, and inference cost grows with N. So it is often worth training a smaller model on much more data than the compute-optimal amount.

Estimating training cost. A forward pass costs about 2N floating-point operations per token: each weight takes part in one multiply and one add. The backward pass costs about twice the forward pass. So

C  ≈  6 N Dfloating-point operations.C \;\approx\; 6\,N\,D \quad \text{floating-point operations.}

Checkpoint 13.1. Your Chapter 12 model has about 8 × 10^5 parameters and trained for 3000 steps of 4096 tokens. Estimate its training computation with the 6ND rule. How many tokens per parameter did it see? By the Chinchilla rule, was it oversized or undersized for its data?

#13.5 Emergence, and a caution

Although loss falls smoothly with scale, performance on particular tasks sometimes does not. Small models score at chance on, say, multi-digit arithmetic or a multi-step reasoning task, and then accuracy rises sharply past some model size. Such abilities have been called emergent.

Be careful with the word. Part of the abruptness comes from the metric. Exact-match accuracy on a five-token answer requires all five tokens to be right; if per-token accuracy improves smoothly, the product rises like a sharp threshold (Schaeffer and others, 2023). With finer-grained metrics many apparent jumps become gradual. What is well supported is the practical point: you cannot list a larger model's capabilities by extrapolating task accuracy from smaller ones, and new capabilities have repeatedly been discovered after training, not designed in.

#13.6 In-context learning

The most surprising capability to appear with scale is in-context learning. Put a few input-output examples of a task in the prompt, follow them with a new input, and the model produces the corresponding output. No parameters change. The model has, within one forward pass, inferred the task from the examples and applied it.

Why would next-token prediction produce this? Training text is full of local patterns: a list in a consistent format, a table, a sequence of questions and answers, a variable used consistently through a program. To predict the later part of such a document, the best strategy is to infer the pattern from the earlier part. A model trained on enough documents is therefore trained, implicitly, to infer patterns on the fly. It has been optimized to learn from its context.

The mechanism is an active research area. Three findings are reasonably secure.

  • Transformers trained from scratch on synthetic tasks can learn to perform regression on examples given in their context, matching classical estimators (Garg and others, 2022).

  • Attention layers can represent a step of gradient descent on a loss defined by the in-context examples, and trained models sometimes approximate this (von Oswald and others, 2023).

  • Small circuits of attention heads that find an earlier occurrence of the current token and copy what followed it, called induction heads, appear during training and account for simple in-context pattern completion (Chapter 19).

The result is a system with two kinds of learning on two timescales. Learning in the weights is slow, happens by gradient descent, and is permanent. Learning in the context is fast, happens by forward computation, and vanishes when the context is cleared. Chapter 20 considers what to make of this.

#13.7 Engineering at scale

A frontier training run occupies tens of thousands of accelerators for weeks to months. The algorithm is Chapter 12's. The difficulty is making it run.

TechniqueWhat it does
Mixed precisionCompute in 16-bit floats, keeping sensitive accumulations in 32-bit. Halves memory and speeds up arithmetic
Data parallelismSplit each batch across devices; each computes gradients on its share; gradients are averaged
Model parallelismSplit the model itself across devices, by layer or within layers, when it does not fit on one
Gradient accumulationSum gradients over several small batches before one update, to emulate a large batch
Activation checkpointingStore only some activations and recompute the rest during the backward pass. Trades computation for memory
Memory-efficient attentionCompute exact attention in blocks without ever storing the full T × T matrix
Checkpointing and restartSave state regularly. At this scale hardware failures are routine events

Memory arithmetic. Three things occupy memory during training.

  1. The parameters: N values.

  2. The optimizer state: for Adam, two more values per parameter, plus the gradient itself.

  3. The activations kept for the backward pass, proportional to batch size × sequence length × width × depth, plus the attention matrices.

At 4 bytes per value, parameters, gradients, and Adam's two moments cost about 16N bytes before any activations. A model with 7 billion parameters needs over 100 gigabytes to train and 14 gigabytes to store in 16-bit precision. Inference needs only the parameters and a small working set.

#13.8 Refinements at scale

The architecture of Chapter 11 has changed little since 2017. The changes that have become common:

ComponentCommon current choice
PositionRotary embeddings (Section 11.6)
NormalizationPre-norm, often RMSNorm
MLPA gated variant with a smooth activation, such as SwiGLU
AttentionFewer key and value heads shared among query heads, to shrink the inference cache (Chapter 15)
SparsityMixture of experts: each block holds several MLPs and a learned router sends each token to a few of them, so only part of the model is active per token
ContextWindows from 10^5 to 10^6 tokens

None of these changes the picture. They are worth knowing when reading papers, and none is required to understand how the models work.

#13.9 What scale has and has not bought

A fair summary, to be refined in Chapter 17.

Bought: fluent text in many languages and styles; broad recall of facts that are well represented in the training data; working code; in-context learning; a substantial degree of multi-step reasoning, especially when the model writes its intermediate steps.

Not bought: reliable factual accuracy; awareness of the limits of its own knowledge; knowledge of events after the training data ends; learning from experience across conversations, without added machinery; consistent behavior under rephrasing.
