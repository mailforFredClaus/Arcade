7 · Training deep nets

From Neurons to Language Models·10 of 29·10 min read

Objectives. After this chapter you can:

  • explain what normalization layers compute and why layer normalization suits sequence models;

  • explain residual connections through the product of Jacobians;

  • apply dropout, weight decay, label smoothing, and early stopping, and say when each is appropriate;

  • diagnose a training run from its loss curves;

  • follow a systematic procedure for debugging a model that does not train.

#7.1 The obstacle

By 2014 the pieces from the previous chapters were in place: ReLU, good initialization, momentum, GPUs. Networks of ten or twenty layers trained well. Networks of fifty or a hundred did not. And the failure was not overfitting. Deeper plain networks had higher training error than shallower ones, which should be impossible if the optimizer were doing its job, since the deeper network could copy the shallower one and set its extra layers to the identity.

The cause is the one identified in Section 5.5: forward signals and backward gradients are long products of per-layer factors, and long products are unstable. Two ideas from 2015 fixed this. Together with attention they are the structural ingredients of every modern network.

#7.2 Normalization

A normalization layer rescales its input to have controlled statistics, then applies a learned scale and shift so that no representational power is lost.

Batch normalization (Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015) standardizes each feature using its mean and variance across the current minibatch:

z^=z−μBσB2+ϵ,y=γ z^+β.\hat z = \frac{z - \mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y = \gamma\,\hat z + \beta .

Here γ and β are learned, one pair per feature. At inference, running averages of the statistics collected during training replace the batch statistics.

Batch normalization made much deeper networks trainable and allowed much larger learning rates. Why it works is less settled than its success. The original explanation, that it reduces shifts in the distribution of layer inputs, has been disputed. A better-supported account is that it makes the loss surface smoother and decouples the scale of each layer's weights from the scale of its output (Santurkar and others, 2018). What is not in dispute: it keeps activations in a sane range at every depth, whatever the weights do.

Its weakness is the dependence on the batch. Each example's output depends on the other examples in the batch, behavior differs between training and inference, and small batches give noisy statistics. For sequences of varying length it is awkward.

Layer normalization (Ba, Kiros, and Hinton, 2016) removes the dependence by normalizing across the features of a single example instead of across the batch. For a vector x ∈ R^d:

LN(x)  =  γ⊙x−μ(x)σ2(x)+ϵ+β,μ(x)=1d∑i=1dxi,σ2(x)=1d∑i=1d(xi−μ(x))2.\mathrm{LN}(x) \;=\; \gamma \odot \frac{x - \mu(x)}{\sqrt{\sigma^2(x) + \epsilon}} + \beta, \qquad \mu(x) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i, \quad \sigma^2(x) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d} (x_i - \mu(x))^2 .

In a Transformer this is applied to each token's vector independently. It behaves identically in training and inference and does not care about batch size. A simplified variant, RMSNorm, skips the mean subtraction and divides by the root mean square of the entries; many recent LLMs use it.

Batch normThe batch, per featureYesConvolutional networks

#7.3 Residual connections

A residual connection (He, Zhang, Ren, and Sun, 2015) adds a layer's input to its output:

hl+1  =  hl+Fl(hl).h_{l+1} \;=\; h_l + F_l(h_l).

Figure 7.2: the block's layers sit on a side branch. Whatever they compute is added to a copy of the input that passed straight through.

The block F_l learns a correction to its input instead of a full replacement. Three consequences follow.

The identity is the default. If F_l outputs zero, the block does nothing. A deeper network can now trivially represent a shallower one, so extra depth cannot hurt in principle, and in practice it stops hurting.

Gradients have a direct path. The Jacobian of one block is I + J_l, where J_l is the Jacobian of F_l. Through a stack of blocks the backward product is

∂hL∂h0  =  (I+JL−1)(I+JL−2)⋯(I+J0)  =  I  +  ∑lJl  +  ∑l>kJlJk  +  ⋯\frac{\partial h_L}{\partial h_0} \;=\; (I + J_{L-1})(I + J_{L-2})\cdots(I + J_0) \;=\; I \;+\; \sum_{l} J_l \;+\; \sum_{l > k} J_l J_k \;+\; \cdots

Expanding gives a sum over all subsets of blocks, and the first term is the identity. Whatever the J_l are, the gradient at the output reaches every earlier layer undiminished along that term. A plain network has only the last term, the full product, which is the one that vanishes.

A shared stream. Unrolling the recursion, h_L = h_0 + F_0(h_0) + F_1(h_1) + ... + F_(L−1)(h_(L−1)). The final representation is the input plus the sum of every block's contribution. It is useful to think of h as a running state, the residual stream, that each block reads from and writes an update into. This view becomes central for Transformers (Chapter 11) and for interpreting them (Chapter 19).

The stream has a cost: contributions accumulate, so its magnitude tends to grow with depth unless something restrains it. That something is normalization, or initializing each block's final layer near zero.

#7.4 The two together: a measurement

Lab 7 trains ReLU MLPs of width 64 on a small digit-recognition task for 300 steps, with and without each ingredient. Two numbers are reported: the ratio of first-layer to last-layer gradient norm at initialization, and the training loss afterward. The loss of a model that has learned nothing is log 10 = 2.303.

At depth 5 every properly initialized variant works. At depth 50 the picture is sharper.

  • The default initialization leaves the first layer with a gradient seventeen orders of magnitude smaller than the last. Nothing is learned.

  • He initialization fixes the gradient scale at the start, and training proceeds, slowly.

  • Normalization alone lets gradients grow toward the input, and training barely moves.

  • Residual connections alone keep gradients healthy, but the stream's magnitude roughly doubles per block, the logits become astronomically large, and the loss blows up (Problem 7.6).

  • With both, the 50-layer network trains about as well as the 5-layer one.

This is one seed, one learning rate, and a short run. It illustrates the mechanisms and is not a benchmark. Run it yourself and vary the settings.

#7.5 Regularization for deep networks

Weight decay. As in Section 6.4, through AdamW.

Dropout (Srivastava and others, 2014). During training, set each activation to zero independently with probability p, and scale the survivors by 1/(1 − p) so the expected value is unchanged. At inference, do nothing. A unit can no longer rely on any particular other unit being present, which discourages fragile co-adapted features. Equivalently, each step trains a different random subnetwork, and the full network at inference approximates their average. Typical values are 0.1 in Transformers and up to 0.5 in older fully connected layers.

Data augmentation. Apply transformations that should not change the label, such as crops and flips for images, and train on the results. This injects knowledge of invariances and enlarges the effective dataset.

Label smoothing. Replace the one-hot target with a mixture: 1 − ε on the correct class and ε spread uniformly. Cross-entropy with a one-hot target is minimized only by infinite logits. Smoothing makes the optimum finite and discourages overconfidence (Problem 7.5).

Early stopping. Keep the checkpoint with the best validation loss.

Scale. In LLM pretraining each token is seen about once, so there is little opportunity to memorize the training set, and training loss tracks held-out loss closely. Explicit regularization matters less in that regime. The dataset is the regularizer.

#7.6 Reading loss curves

Always plot training and validation loss against step. Most problems have a signature.

What you seeLikely causeWhat to try
Loss stuck at log K from the first stepNo learning signal: a data or label bug, dead initialization, or a learning rate off by orders of magnitudeOverfit one batch; inspect inputs and labels; check per-layer gradient norms
Loss becomes NaN or shoots upLearning rate too high; log of zero; overflowLower the rate; add clipping; compute losses from logits

#7.7 A debugging procedure

Neural networks fail silently. A model with a bug usually still trains, just worse, and nothing raises an error. Discipline substitutes for error messages.

  1. Look at the data. Print raw examples and labels exactly as the model receives them, after all preprocessing. A large share of bugs are found here.

  2. Check the initial loss. It should be log K for a K-way classifier. If not, the output layer or the loss is wrong.

  3. Overfit one small batch. Train on the same 8 to 32 examples repeatedly. The loss must go to nearly zero. If it does not, there is a bug in the model or the optimization, and no amount of data or tuning will help.

  4. Check gradient flow. Print the gradient norm of every layer. They should be within a few orders of magnitude of each other. A useful second check: the ratio of the update size to the weight size, per layer, should be around 1e-3.

  5. Establish a simple baseline. Fix the random seed. Get a small model working end to end before adding anything.

  6. Add capacity, then regularize. First make the model big enough to overfit. Then pull the validation loss down.

  7. Change one thing at a time, and write down what you changed and what happened.

Step 3 is the most valuable single test in this book. Use it every time.

#7.8 Hyperparameter search

Search on a log scale for anything multiplicative: learning rate, weight decay. Random search beats grid search when only a few hyperparameters matter, because a grid wastes trials repeating the same values of the important ones (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012). Order of importance for most problems: learning rate, then model size and batch size, then schedule length, then regularization strength.

#Lab 7: what makes depth trainable

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits

class Layer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, norm, residual):
        super().__init__()
        self.lin = nn.Linear(d, d)
        self.norm = nn.LayerNorm(d) if norm else nn.Identity()
        self.residual = residual
    def forward(self, x):
        h = F.relu(self.lin(self.norm(x)))
        return x + h if self.residual else h

def make_net(depth, d=64, norm=False, residual=False, he_init=False):
    net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, d), *[Layer(d, norm, residual) for _ in range(depth)],
                        nn.Linear(d, 10))
    if he_init:
        for m in net.modules():
            if isinstance(m, nn.Linear):
                nn.init.kaiming_normal_(m.weight, nonlinearity="relu"); nn.init.zeros_(m.bias)
    return net

digits = load_digits()
X = torch.tensor(digits.data, dtype=torch.float32) / 16.0      # 1797 images, 8x8, flattened
y = torch.tensor(digits.target)

variants = {
    "plain, default init": dict(),
    "plain, He init":      dict(he_init=True),
    "+ LayerNorm":         dict(he_init=True, norm=True),
    "+ residual":          dict(he_init=True, residual=True),
    "+ both":              dict(he_init=True, norm=True, residual=True),
}
for depth in [5, 50]:
    print(f"depth {depth}")
    for name, kw in variants.items():
        torch.manual_seed(0)
        net = make_net(depth, **kw)
        loss = F.cross_entropy(net(X), y); loss.backward()
        g_first = net[1].lin.weight.grad.norm().item()          # first hidden layer
        g_last = net[depth].lin.weight.grad.norm().item()       # last hidden layer
        opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
        for step in range(300):
            idx = torch.randint(0, len(X), (128,))
            loss = F.cross_entropy(net(X[idx]), y[idx])
            opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
        final = F.cross_entropy(net(X), y).item()
        print(f"  {name:20s} grad first/last at init {g_first / g_last:9.2e}   loss after 300 steps {final:.3f}")

The digit data ships with scikit-learn, so nothing is downloaded. This is the first lab in PyTorch. If the framework is new to you, read the code against Lab 5A: nn.Linear holds W and b, loss.backward() is the backward pass, and opt.step() is the parameter update.
