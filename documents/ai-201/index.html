#Part I — Inside the Machine

#1. Transformer anatomy with the math

A decoder-only transformer is a stack of identical blocks. Each block reads from and adds to one vector per token, called the residual stream. Attention moves information between tokens, and the feed-forward network transforms each token's vector; the other parts keep training stable.

In control-diagram terms, each sub-layer is a branch whose output is summed back into the stream. Nothing overwrites the stream, so information from early layers survives to late ones.

Attention, precisely

For one head, stack the sequence's token vectors as the rows of X:

Q=XWQ,K=XWK,V=XWV,Attn(Q,K,V)=softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk+M)VQ = XW_Q,\quad K = XW_K,\quad V = XW_V,\qquad \mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\Big)V

  • M is the causal mask: zero on and below the diagonal and −∞ above it, so token i sees only tokens up to i.

  • Dividing by √d_k keeps the scores' variance near 1, so the softmax does not saturate.

  • Each softmax row sums to 1. Token i takes a weighted average of earlier tokens' value vectors, weighted by query–key similarity.

  • Heads run in parallel on 128-dimensional slices. W_O projects their concatenated outputs back to the model width. Different heads learn different relations, such as the previous token, a matching bracket or the subject of a sentence.

Modern attention variants differ mainly in how many key-value heads they keep, which sets the size of the KV cache:

Position as rotation: RoPE

Attention by itself ignores word order. Rotary position embedding (RoPE) splits q and k into 2-D pairs and rotates pair i by the angle mθᵢ at position m (RoFormer):

θi=b−2i/d,q~m=R(mθ) q,k~n=R(nθ) k    ⇒    q~m⊤k~n=q⊤R((n−m)θ) k\theta_i = b^{-2i/d},\qquad \tilde q_m = R(m\theta)\,q,\quad \tilde k_n = R(n\theta)\,k \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \tilde q_m^{\top}\tilde k_n = q^{\top} R\big((n-m)\theta\big)\,k

Because rotations compose, the score depends only on the offset n − m. Each pair behaves like a phasor at its own frequency. Fast pairs track word order; slow ones track position across thousands of tokens. The base b runs from 10,000 to 1,000,000 or more in long-context models. Chapter 5 covers stretching RoPE past its training length.

Normalization and the feed-forward network

x←x+Attn(RMSNorm(x)),x←x+FFN(RMSNorm(x))x \leftarrow x + \mathrm{Attn}\big(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x)\big),\qquad x \leftarrow x + \mathrm{FFN}\big(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x)\big)
RMSNorm(x)=g⊙x1d∑jxj2+ϵ,FFN(x)=W2(SiLU(W1x)⊙W3x)\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x) = g \odot \frac{x}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{d}\sum_j x_j^2 + \epsilon}},\qquad \mathrm{FFN}(x) = W_2\big(\mathrm{SiLU}(W_1x)\odot W_3x\big)

  • Pre-norm residuals normalize each branch's input rather than the stream itself, which keeps very deep stacks trainable.

  • RMSNorm rescales by the root-mean-square and skips LayerNorm's mean subtraction; g is a learned gain.

  • SwiGLU multiplies two projections elementwise, where SiLU(z) = z·σ(z). One projection acts as a learned, input-dependent gate on the other. The hidden width is usually 3–5× the model width: 25,600, or 5×, in the example.

  • Mixture of experts replaces the FFN with E experts and a router that picks the top k for each token. The output is Σ gᵢ(x)·FFNᵢ(x) over the chosen experts, and a balancing term keeps the experts evenly loaded.

Each parameter does one multiply-add per token in the forward pass, so the forward pass costs about 2N FLOPs per token: 62 GFLOPs here. Training costs about 6N per token, since the backward pass costs twice the forward. Attention adds compute that grows with context length L:

At about L = 30,000 tokens, attention costs as much as all the weight matmuls together. Past that point it dominates. This is one reason long-context prefill is slow, and why Chapter 5's alternatives exist.

The output end, and what training minimizes

A final norm and the output head turn the stream into logits over the vocabulary. Training minimizes cross-entropy, −log p(the actual next token), averaged over every position of every sequence. Perplexity is exp(loss). For intuition, a loss of 2.0 nats means the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among e² ≈ 7.4 tokens.

#2. Scaling laws and the economics of pretraining

To first order, pretraining is predictable. Compute is about 6ND FLOPs for N parameters and D training tokens, and loss falls as a power law in both. Those two facts explain model sizes, training budgets, and why small models are now trained far past the compute-optimal point.

The compute equation

C≈6ND,L(N,D)≈E+ANα+BDβC \approx 6ND,\qquad L(N,D) \approx E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}

The loss fit comes from DeepMind's Chinchilla study. E is the irreducible loss, and the fitted exponents α and β are both near 0.3 (Hoffmann et al.). As a worked example, train a 70B model on 1.4 trillion tokens:

  1. Compute: C = 6 × 7×10¹⁰ × 1.4×10¹² ≈ 5.9×10²³ FLOPs.

  2. Throughput: an H100 is rated at about 990 TFLOPS dense BF16. At a typical 40% model-FLOPs utilization (MFU), it delivers about 4×10¹⁴ FLOP/s.

  3. Time: 1.5×10⁹ GPU-seconds, or about 410,000 GPU-hours. At $2–4 an hour that is roughly $0.8–1.6 million.

Frontier programs use many more parameters and tokens, run many experimental models, and add reinforcement learning on top. Their costs are not published, which is why figures for them are estimates.

Compute-optimal versus inference-optimal

  • Kaplan et al. (2020) found smooth power laws across seven orders of magnitude and favored growing N faster than D (paper).

  • Chinchilla (2022) corrected this: at fixed compute, N and D should grow together, at about 20 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla, a 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens, beat the 280B Gopher with the same compute.

  • Inference-optimal training. Training is paid once, but inference is paid on every token served. So labs "overtrain" small models. Llama 3 8B saw over 15 trillion tokens, about 1,900 per parameter or 95× Chinchilla's ratio (Llama 3).

  • The data wall. Good text is finite. Repeating data for up to about four epochs is nearly as good as fresh data, with diminishing returns after that (Muennighoff et al.). Synthetic data and RL environments are the industry's answer.

Memory: why training needs clusters

Mixed-precision AdamW needs about 16 bytes per parameter:

  • 2 bytes of BF16 weights

  • 2 bytes of BF16 gradients

  • 4 bytes of FP32 master weights

  • 8 bytes for Adam's two moment estimates

A 70B model therefore needs 1.12 TB before any activations. Activations grow with batch size, sequence length, width and depth. Activation checkpointing recomputes them during the backward pass, which costs about a third more compute and saves most of the memory.

Precision and optimizers

  • BF16 keeps FP32's exponent range with a short mantissa, so it needs no loss scaling. It is the training default.

  • FP8 training works at scale with fine-grained scaling factors. DeepSeek-V3 was trained this way (DeepSeek-V3).

  • AdamW gives each parameter its own adaptive step size, with decoupled weight decay. A warmup is followed by a cosine or warmup-stable-decay schedule.

  • Muon orthogonalizes the momentum update of each weight matrix with a few Newton–Schulz iterations. Moonshot reported that it scales to large models (Muon is scalable).

  • Stability tricks: gradient clipping, normalizing queries and keys, and an auxiliary loss on the logits. Long runs still restart from checkpoints after loss spikes.

What the data mixture does

Pretraining data is web text filtered by quality classifiers and deduplicated, with code and math upsampled, plus books, papers and many languages. It increasingly includes synthetic data: rephrased documents, textbook-style explanations, and generated problems with verified answers. Near the end of training, labs anneal the learning rate on the highest-quality data, which lifts benchmark scores noticeably. Tokenizer vocabularies of 100,000–260,000 entries trade fewer tokens per text against a larger embedding matrix.

Inference-time compute is cheap in FLOPs, expensive in time

A 10,000-token reasoning trace on the 32B example costs 10,000 × 62 GFLOPs = 6.2×10¹⁴ FLOPs, about 1.5 seconds of one H100 at 40% MFU. At batch size 1, though, it takes minutes, because decoding is limited by memory bandwidth rather than compute. That mismatch between FLOPs and time is the subject of Chapter 4.

#3. Post-training in depth

Post-training turns a next-token predictor into an assistant by optimizing it against rewards: human preferences, AI judgments against written principles, and automatic verifiers. The math is short. Much of what you see in daily use follows from it, including strength in code, flattery, and agents that game tests.

Supervised fine-tuning

Supervised fine-tuning uses the same cross-entropy loss as pretraining, computed only on the assistant's tokens in curated conversations. It teaches format, tool-call syntax and conversational style. Because it imitates its demonstrations, it copies their mistakes and cannot exceed them.

Reward models and RLHF

A reward model rφ learns from human comparisons of two answers, y_w (preferred) and y_l. The Bradley–Terry model gives the probability of that preference, and the policy is then optimized against the reward, held near a reference model:

P(yw≻yl∣x)=σ(rϕ(x,yw)−rϕ(x,yl)),max⁡π  Ey∼π[rϕ(x,y)]−β KL(π(⋅∣x) ∥ πref(⋅∣x))P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big),\qquad \max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)

The KL term is a leash. It keeps the policy close to the fine-tuned model, so it cannot drift into text the reward model scores highly but was never trained to judge. InstructGPT optimized this with PPO, which keeps four models in memory: policy, reference, reward and value (InstructGPT).

DPO: preferences without a reward model

LDPO=−log⁡σ ⁣(β[log⁡π(yw∣x)πref(yw∣x)−log⁡π(yl∣x)πref(yl∣x)])\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\!\left(\beta\left[\log\frac{\pi(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right]\right)

The optimal RLHF policy has a closed form, so the reward can be written in terms of the policy itself. The preference pairs then train the policy directly, much like a classification loss (DPO). DPO is simple and stable, which makes it popular for open models and home fine-tuning (Chapter 15). Its limit is that it is offline: it learns from fixed pairs, never from the model's own fresh attempts.

GRPO and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

For each prompt, GRPO samples a group of G answers, scores each with a verifier, and normalizes the scores within the group:

Ai=ri−mean⁡(r1,…,rG)std⁡(r1,…,rG)A_i = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}

A PPO-style clipped update with a KL penalty follows. There is no value network; the group mean serves as the baseline (DeepSeekMath). When the rewards come from unit tests, exact-match answers or proof checkers, this is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR).

In simulation terms, the policy gradient is a score-function Monte Carlo estimator, ∇E[r] = E[r ∇log π], and the group mean is a control variate that cuts its variance. DeepSeek-R1-Zero applied RL of this kind directly to a base model. Long reasoning chains with self-checking and backtracking emerged without anyone demonstrating them (DeepSeek-R1).

Outcome versus process rewards

Outcome rewards grade only the final answer; process reward models grade each step. On competition math, OpenAI found that supervising the process beat supervising the outcome (Let's Verify Step by Step). In agent training, intermediate checks act as the process signal: whether the code compiles, or which tests now pass. Assigning credit across hundreds of tool calls remains a hard, open problem.

Constitutional AI and AI feedback

In Anthropic's Constitutional AI, the model critiques and revises its own outputs against written principles. A preference model trained on AI comparisons then drives the RL stage, replacing most human harmlessness labels (Constitutional AI). This scales feedback and makes the target values explicit and inspectable.

Agentic RL environments

Much of the 2025–2026 progress in coding and agents comes from RL inside sandboxes: repositories with test suites, terminals, browsers and tool APIs. The bottleneck is environment design. Tasks must be verifiable, varied and hard to game, and a single exploitable checker teaches the exploit.

#4. Inference engineering

An LLM's speed is set by data movement, not arithmetic. Each generated token reads every active weight from memory once, so decoding a single stream uses only a few percent of a GPU's compute. Batching, paged KV caches, speculative decoding and quantization all exist to do more useful work per byte moved.

The roofline model

Pattainable=min⁡(Ppeak,  β⋅I),I=FLOPsbytes movedP_{\text{attainable}} = \min\big(P_{\text{peak}},\; \beta \cdot I\big),\qquad I = \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{bytes moved}}

Here β is memory bandwidth and I is arithmetic intensity. During single-stream decoding, each weight is read once per token and used for 2 FLOPs per parameter. That gives I ≈ 1 FLOP per byte at 16 bits and about 4 at 4 bits. A 4090-class GPU needs about 164 FLOPs per byte to keep its tensor cores busy.

The two single-stream points sit at the bottom left. Running 32 streams lets them share each weight read, and prefill reuses each weight across every prompt token, so both climb toward the compute roof.

Batching and the KV cache

  • Batching multiplies intensity by the batch size B. At 4 bits, about 40 streams reach the ridge. Each stream's KV cache is still read on every step and cannot be shared, so long contexts erode the gain.

  • The memory budget decides how much you can batch. The 32B example at about 4.5 bits per weight needs 18 GB, leaving about 5 GB of a 24 GB card. At 256 KB per token that holds about 20,000 tokens of context across all streams, or twice that with an 8-bit cache.

  • PagedAttention stores the KV cache in fixed-size blocks with a block table per sequence, like virtual-memory pages. Earlier servers lost most of their KV memory to fragmentation; vLLM cut the waste to under 4% (vLLM). Blocks can also be shared between requests with the same prompt prefix, which is the mechanism behind API prompt caching.

  • Continuous batching admits and retires sequences at every step instead of waiting for a whole batch to finish.

FlashAttention

Naive attention writes the full L × L score matrix to GPU memory. FlashAttention instead streams blocks of K and V through on-chip SRAM and keeps a running softmax, so the full matrix never exists (FlashAttention). The result is exact, with memory linear in L and far less traffic. The running softmax is the stable streaming log-sum-exp; as each new score s arrives, it updates the running max m and normalizer ℓ:

m′=max⁡(m,s),ℓ′=ℓ e m−m′+e s−m′m' = \max(m, s),\qquad \ell' = \ell\, e^{\,m - m'} + e^{\,s - m'}

Speculative decoding

A small draft model proposes γ tokens, and the target model checks all of them in one forward pass, which costs about the same as generating one token. Each drafted token x is accepted with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)), where p and q are the target's and draft's probabilities. On a rejection, the next token is resampled from the normalized remainder max(0, p − q). The output distribution is exactly the target model's (Leviathan et al., Chen et al.). With a per-token acceptance rate α:

E[tokens per target pass]=1−αγ+11−α,α=0.8, γ=4  ⇒  3.36\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per target pass}] = \frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{1-\alpha},\qquad \alpha = 0.8,\ \gamma = 4 \;\Rightarrow\; 3.36

Variants replace the draft model with extra prediction heads trained alongside the main model (multi-token prediction, as in DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen3.8), or with n-gram lookup. N-gram lookup works well for code edits, where the output copies large parts of the input.

Quantization, mechanically

The basic scheme is block-wise absmax scaling, where q_max is the largest integer the format can store:

s=max⁡i∈block∣wi∣qmax⁡,qi=round⁡ ⁣(wis),w^i=s qis = \frac{\max_{i \in \text{block}} |w_i|}{q_{\max}},\qquad q_i = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{w_i}{s}\right),\qquad \hat w_i = s\, q_i

  • Block size sets the error. The rounding error per weight is at most s/2, and smaller blocks give tighter scales. GGUF k-quants nest scales inside super-blocks; MXFP4 shares a power-of-two scale across 32 weights; NVFP4 uses blocks of 16 with an FP8 scale.

  • Weights are easy; activations are hard. A few activation channels carry outliers far larger than the rest, which wreck any shared scale (LLM.int8()). Consumer quantization is therefore mostly weight-only: weights stored at 4 bits and expanded to 16 bits on the fly. Because decoding is memory-bound, the extra arithmetic costs almost nothing.

  • Rounding can be smarter than nearest. GPTQ uses second-order information to adjust the remaining weights after each rounding error (GPTQ). AWQ rescales the channels that matter most to activations before quantizing (AWQ). llama.cpp's importance matrix plays a similar role.

  • There is a cliff. Quality holds well down to about 4 bits, then falls quickly. Larger models tolerate low precision better than small ones.

Why API output tokens cost five times input

Prefill runs in parallel and uses compute efficiently. Decoding is sequential, memory-bound, and occupies KV-cache memory for the whole generation. Every current Claude model prices output at 5× input (pricing). Large providers also run prefill and decode on separate GPU pools, called disaggregated serving, so each phase can run near its own roofline.

#5. Long context and state-space models

Attention's compute grows with the square of context length, its memory grows linearly, and models are usually trained on shorter sequences than they serve. There are three families of fixes: stretch the position encoding, restrict which tokens attend to which, or replace attention with a recurrent state. Each one trades some exact recall for lower cost.

Stretching positions

A RoPE model behaves badly past its training length. Its slowest-rotating dimension pairs reach angles it has never seen. The fixes rescale positions:

  • Position interpolation squeezes position m by L_train / L_target.

  • NTK-aware scaling changes the base b instead.

  • YaRN interpolates the slow pairs, leaves the fast ones alone, and tempers the attention scores (YaRN).

A short continued training on long documents follows. For example, Qwen3-32B is trained for 32,768 tokens and reaches 131,072 with YaRN (model card). Local engines expose these as RoPE-scaling options; enable them only when the model card says to.

Restricting attention

  • Sliding-window attention lets each token see only the last W tokens, so cost grows as L·W. Google's Gemma models interleave several local layers with each global layer.

  • Attention sinks. Models put large attention weight on the first few tokens. Streaming methods keep those tokens plus a recent window, and stay stable over very long inputs (StreamingLLM).

Replacing attention with a state: the filter view

Mamba is a selective state-space model (Mamba):

ht=Aˉt ht−1+Bˉt xt,yt=Ct hth_t = \bar A_t\, h_{t-1} + \bar B_t\, x_t,\qquad y_t = C_t\, h_t

The matrices are computed from the input at each step, which is what "selective" means. This is a linear time-varying state-space system, the same form as a Kalman filter's propagation step. Its state has a fixed size, so memory and time per token stay constant however long the input grows.

Gated DeltaNet keeps a matrix state S that maps keys to values and updates it with the delta rule (Gated DeltaNet):

St=αt St−1(I−βtktkt⊤)+βt vtkt⊤,ot=St qtS_t = \alpha_t\, S_{t-1}\big(I - \beta_t k_t k_t^{\top}\big) + \beta_t\, v_t k_t^{\top},\qquad o_t = S_t\, q_t

Expanded, this is S_{t−1} + β_t (v_t − S_{t−1}k_t) k_tᵀ: a correction of the prediction error for the new key. In other words, it is the Widrow–Hoff LMS adaptive-filter update with a forgetting factor α_t, so the layer performs online least squares as it reads.

The trade-off is that a fixed state compresses history. It keeps the gist well but is weaker at verbatim recall of a detail from 100,000 tokens back. Hybrids therefore keep a few full-attention layers for exact lookup. Qwen3.8 mixes DeltaNet and attention, and NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 mixes Mamba-2 layers with mixture-of-experts.

Advertised versus effective context

  • Lost in the middle. Accuracy follows a U-shape: facts at the start or end of the context are found more reliably than facts in the middle (Liu et al.).

  • RULER. Harder synthetic tasks than needle-in-a-haystack showed effective lengths well below advertised ones (RULER).

  • Context rot. Chroma's 2025 study found performance falling as inputs grew, even on simple tasks, across many frontier models (Chroma).

Today's 1M-token models are far better than those of 2023, but the rule stands: test your own task at your own length (Chapter 9).

#6. Diffusion and flow models, mathematically

An image or video generator learns a vector field that carries noise to data, and sampling integrates that field. Seen as an ODE, every ComfyUI setting maps onto numerical-methods vocabulary you already know.

The forward process: adding noise

Diffusion training corrupts a clean image x₀ with Gaussian noise at a random level t and trains a network to predict the noise:

xt=αˉt x0+1−αˉt ϵ,ϵ∼N(0,I),L=E ∥ϵ−ϵθ(xt,t,c)∥2x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\qquad \mathcal L = \mathbb E\,\big\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)\big\|^2

Here c is the prompt's conditioning (DDPM). Predicting the noise is equivalent, up to scale, to estimating the score ∇log p_t(x). That links the method to stochastic differential equations and to a deterministic "probability-flow" ODE with the same marginals (Song et al.).

Flow matching: the straight-line version

Most current models, including FLUX, Stable Diffusion 3, Qwen-Image and Wan, use flow matching. It interpolates linearly between data and noise and regresses the constant velocity along that line:

xt=(1−t) x0+t ϵ,L=E ∥vθ(xt,t,c)−(ϵ−x0)∥2,dxdt=vθ(x,t,c)x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,\epsilon,\qquad \mathcal L = \mathbb E\,\big\|v_\theta(x_t,t,c) - (\epsilon - x_0)\big\|^2,\qquad \frac{dx}{dt} = v_\theta(x,t,c)

Sampling integrates the ODE from t = 1 (pure noise) back to t = 0 (the image) (Flow Matching, Rectified Flow). Straighter paths need fewer integration steps, which is the whole appeal.

Classifier-free guidance

The model is trained with the prompt randomly dropped, so it learns both conditional and unconditional fields. Sampling extrapolates from one toward the other with guidance scale w (CFG):

v~=vθ(x,t,∅)+w (vθ(x,t,c)−vθ(x,t,∅))\tilde v = v_\theta(x,t,\varnothing) + w\,\big(v_\theta(x,t,c) - v_\theta(x,t,\varnothing)\big)

A w above 1 tightens prompt adherence but oversaturates at high values, and it costs two model evaluations per step. A negative prompt simply replaces the empty condition ∅. Guidance-distilled models bake the effect into one pass. That is why many recent models run at CFG 1 and take a separate guidance value instead.

Latents and diffusion transformers

A VAE compresses the image first, typically 8× in each spatial direction into 16 channels. A 1024 × 1024 image becomes a 128 × 128 × 16 latent. Today's denoisers are diffusion transformers: the latent is cut into 2 × 2 patches, giving 64 × 64 = 4,096 tokens that attend jointly with the text tokens (Latent Diffusion, DiT). Doubling the image's side length gives 4× the tokens and up to 16× the attention compute, which is why most models work best near one megapixel.

Video: why clips are short

Video VAEs also compress time, typically 4×. Take 81 frames at 1280 × 720, five seconds at 16 frames per second. The latent is 21 × 90 × 160, and 2 × 2 patches turn it into 21 × 45 × 80 = 75,600 tokens. Attention over 75,600 tokens, at every one of dozens of steps and twice per step with CFG, is why a clip takes minutes. It is also why few-step distilled models and faster attention kernels such as SageAttention matter so much.

Distillation: from 50 steps to 4

  • Consistency models learn to jump from any point on a trajectory straight to its end (Consistency Models).

  • Adversarial distillation adds a discriminator to sharpen few-step outputs; SDXL Turbo used it (ADD).

  • Reflow retrains a model on its own noise–image pairs, which straightens its trajectories.

  • Speed-up LoRAs ("lightning", "turbo") package a distilled few-step model as add-on weights.

The costs are less variety, often no usable CFG, and sometimes less fine detail.

Text encoders are now LLMs

Recent open models condition on a language model's hidden states instead of a small text encoder: Qwen3-VL in FLUX 3 Image Klein, a Mistral model in FLUX.2. That is why they follow long, descriptive prompts. Some closed systems go further and generate images as tokens inside a multimodal LLM, or pair an LLM planner with a diffusion decoder.

#7. Interpretability: what is inside

Interpretability research can now extract human-readable features from production models and trace how those features combine into computations. Its most practical lesson so far is that a model's account of its own reasoning is not a reliable record of how it reached an answer. Verify outputs, not narratives.

Features and superposition

Networks represent far more concepts, or features, than they have dimensions. Each feature gets its own direction, and the network tolerates small interference between nearly orthogonal directions. This is called superposition (Toy Models of Superposition). The nearest engineering analogy is compressed sensing, where sparse signals are recovered from fewer measurements than dimensions. Superposition is also why individual neurons are polysemantic and hard to read.

Sparse autoencoders

A sparse autoencoder is trained on one layer's activations to rewrite each activation vector as a sparse combination of learned feature directions dᵢ:

f(x)=ReLU(Wenc x+b),x^=∑ifi(x) di,min⁡  ∥x−x^∥2+λ ∥f(x)∥1f(x) = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}}\,x + b),\qquad \hat x = \sum_i f_i(x)\, d_i,\qquad \min\; \|x-\hat x\|^2 + \lambda\,\|f(x)\|_1

Anthropic extracted millions of features from Claude 3 Sonnet. Many were interpretable and worked across languages and between text and images. One responded to the Golden Gate Bridge; clamping it high produced "Golden Gate Claude", which steered every conversation toward the bridge. Features tied to deception, sycophancy and security bugs also appeared (Scaling Monosemanticity).

Circuit tracing

Attribution graphs trace which features cause which, through an interpretable stand-in for the model's feed-forward layers (methods, findings). In Claude 3.5 Haiku they showed:

  • Multi-step reasoning inside a single forward pass. "The capital of the state containing Dallas" passes through an internal "Texas" step on the way to "Austin".

  • Planning ahead. When writing rhyming verse, the model picks the rhyme word before it writes the line.

  • Explanations that differ from the computation. The model adds numbers with parallel paths, one for rough magnitude and one for the exact last digit. Asked how it added, it describes the schoolbook carry method.

Chain-of-thought faithfulness

Anthropic slipped hints into prompts for reasoning models. Models that used a hint usually did not mention it in their visible reasoning (Anthropic). Visible thinking is useful for spotting where your prompt misled the model. It is not an audit trail.

What it means for you

  • Treat explanations as hypotheses. A model's explanation of how it got an answer is a claim to check, and the answer needs independent verification either way.

  • Steering and monitoring by feature is an active research area and may become a practical tool. Today its payoff is mostly in labs' safety evaluations.

  • You can explore this yourself. Anthropic released its circuit-tracing tools as open source in 2025, and Neuronpedia hosts interactive feature and circuit explorers for open models.

Reliability engineering for agents

If a run has n steps that each succeed independently with probability p, the whole run succeeds with probability pⁿ:

P(run succeeds)=p n:0.99100≈0.37,0.999100≈0.90P(\text{run succeeds}) = p^{\,n}:\qquad 0.99^{100} \approx 0.37,\qquad 0.999^{100} \approx 0.90

A 1% error rate per step sinks most 100-step runs. The defenses are the ones you would use for any long-running process:

  • Durable state. Persist job IDs and progress after each step, so a crash resumes the run instead of restarting it.

  • Verification gates. Run checks between stages, and do not let the agent declare itself done until they pass. Chapter 12 shows a Claude Code hook that enforces this.

  • A protected checker. Make tests and golden files read-only to the agent.

  • Human approval for irreversible actions: deploys, deletions, emails, purchases.

  • Budgets: turn limits, token or dollar caps, timeouts.

  • Traces. Log every tool call with inputs, outputs, tokens and time, and turn each failure into an eval case (Chapter 9).

#9. Evals as engineering

An eval score is a measurement with an error bar, and the error bar is usually larger than people assume. Twenty tasks can catch a regression. Ranking two good models a few points apart takes hundreds of tasks, a paired design, or both.

The basic error bar

Treat each task as a Bernoulli trial. With n tasks and an observed accuracy p̂:

SE=p^ (1−p^)n,95% interval≈p^±1.96 SE\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p\,(1-\hat p)}{n}},\qquad \text{95\% interval} \approx \hat p \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}

Comparing two models measured on separate task samples is worse still. To resolve a 5-point gap near 70%, the standard error of the difference, √(2p(1 − p)/n), must fall below 0.05/1.96. That takes about 650 tasks per model.

Paired comparisons cut the cost

Run both models on the same tasks and analyze the per-task differences dᵢ = s_A,i − s_B,i:

SE(dˉ)=sd⁡(d)n\mathrm{SE}(\bar d) = \frac{\operatorname{sd}(d)}{\sqrt n}

Tasks that both models pass, or both fail, contribute zero. Only the disagreements carry information; for pass/fail scores this is McNemar's test. Because task difficulty is shared, pairing can cut the number of tasks you need severalfold. Anthropic's guidance on eval statistics also recommends clustered standard errors when tasks come in related groups, such as several questions about one document (Adding Error Bars to Evals).

import numpy as np

a = np.array(scores_a)  # 1 or 0 per task, same task order for both models
b = np.array(scores_b)
d = a - b
se = d.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(d))
print(f"A minus B: {d.mean():+.3f} ± {1.96 * se:.3f} (95% interval)")

Run more than once

Outputs vary from run to run, so a score has two sources of variance: which tasks you chose, and which output each task happened to get. Run each task k times and average. A task that passes half the time is a coin flip, and one run of it proves nothing.

pass@k and pass^k

These two metrics answer different questions:

  • pass@k is the probability that at least one of k attempts succeeds. It matters when you can verify and pick, as with code and tests. Use the unbiased estimator below, with n samples per task of which c are correct (Chen et al.).

  • pass^k is the probability that all k attempts succeed. It matters when an agent runs the same job unattended again and again (τ-bench).

pass@k=E ⁣[1−(n−ck)(nk)],passk=p k\text{pass@}k = \mathbb E\!\left[1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right],\qquad \text{pass}^k = p^{\,k}

A model that succeeds 90% of the time has pass^8 = 0.9⁸ ≈ 0.43: it gets all eight runs right less than half the time.

Using an LLM as judge without fooling yourself

LLM judges have known biases. They favor answers by position, longer answers, and answers from their own model family (Zheng et al.). The countermeasures:

  1. Grade against a rubric of checkable points, one judgment per criterion, rather than asking which answer is better.

  2. Swap the answer order and average the two verdicts.

  3. Use a judge from a different model family than the models under test, where you can.

  4. Calibrate against yourself. Hand-grade 50–100 items and measure agreement with Cohen's κ = (p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e), where p_o is observed and p_e is chance agreement. Trust the judge only where it agrees with you.

  5. Prefer programmatic checks whenever one exists.

Eval-driven development

  • Treat the eval set as the specification. Write the cases before you change a prompt, tool or model.

  • Keep three tiers. A smoke set of 10–20 cases runs on every change; a regression set of 100–300 runs nightly or per release; a frozen holdout you rarely look at guards against tuning prompts to the eval.

  • Turn production failures into new cases.

  • Record cost and latency per case next to the score.

  • Version everything: prompts, model IDs, tool versions and the eval set itself.

  • Grade agents on the final state, meaning files, test results and database rows, rather than the transcript. Check that the agent did not touch the checker.

#10. Production patterns

Code that calls a model needs the same engineering as code that calls any unreliable remote service: schemas, validation, retries, idempotency, timeouts, fallbacks and telemetry. Two concerns are specific to AI: output that is well formed but wrong, and cost that scales with tokens.

Constrained decoding: how structured outputs work

The engine compiles your JSON schema into a grammar, a state machine over the tokenizer's vocabulary. At every step it masks out tokens that would break the grammar, then samples from what remains. The output is guaranteed to parse but not guaranteed to be correct. Local engines offer the same: llama.cpp accepts grammars and JSON schemas, and vLLM and SGLang have structured-output backends.

In the Claude API, output_config.format takes a JSON schema, and strict: true does the same for tool inputs. The limits matter in practice (structured outputs):

  • Schemas need additionalProperties: false.

  • Numeric ranges and string-length constraints are not enforced.

  • At most 20 strict tools per request.

  • Structured outputs cannot be combined with citations.

Put ranges and units in the field descriptions, then validate them yourself.

Validate, then repair

Check in two layers. First the schema, then the meaning: ranges, units, cross-field consistency, whether referenced IDs exist. On failure, send the specific error back once or twice, then fail loudly.

from pydantic import BaseModel, Field, ValidationError

class RunConfig(BaseModel):
    mass_kg: float = Field(gt=0, lt=5000)
    dt_s: float = Field(gt=0, le=0.01)
    integrator: str = Field(pattern="^(rk4|dopri5)$")

def get_config(prompt: str, attempts: int = 3) -> RunConfig:
    feedback = ""
    for _ in range(attempts):
        raw = call_model(prompt + feedback)  # your wrapper, using structured outputs
        try:
            return RunConfig.model_validate_json(raw)
        except ValidationError as e:
            feedback = f"\n\nYour last answer failed validation:\n{e}\nFix only these fields."
    raise RuntimeError("no valid config after retries")

Retries, timeouts, idempotency and fallbacks

  • Retry the right errors. Retry 429 (rate limited), 529 (overloaded) and other 5xx errors with exponential backoff and jitter, and honor retry-after. Do not retry 400-class errors unchanged. The official SDKs retry some of these automatically.

  • Stream long generations rather than holding one request open until a timeout.

  • Make side effects idempotent. Retrying a model call is cheap; repeating a tool's side effect may not be. Give every side-effecting action an idempotency key.

  • Keep a fallback. Put a second model or provider behind the same interface, keep prompts portable, and include the fallback path in your eval suite.

Rate limits

Claude's limits are set per model in requests, input tokens and output tokens per minute. For most models, cache reads do not count toward the input-token limit, so caching raises throughput as well as cutting cost (rate limits). Bulk work belongs in the Batch API, which has its own queue limits.

  • Order content from most to least stable: tools, system prompt, documents, conversation.

Observability

Log these for every call:

  • Model ID and prompt version

  • Input, output and cache tokens

  • Time to first token and total time

  • Stop reason, tool calls and cost

  • A trace ID that links the steps of one agent run

OpenTelemetry defines conventions for generative-AI spans, and Claude Code can export its usage metrics through OpenTelemetry. Prompt logs are sensitive data, so scrub secrets before storing them.

Nondeterminism

Outputs can differ from run to run even with sampling turned all the way down. In served models the main cause is GPU kernels whose floating-point summation order depends on batch size. Your result then depends on which other requests share your batch. Batch-invariant kernels fix this at some cost in speed (Thinking Machines). Design for variance: store outputs you must reproduce, and evaluate over several runs.

#11. Security engineering for AI systems

A model cannot reliably tell instructions from data, so any text it reads can steer it. Security therefore has to come from system design, by limiting what an agent can reach and do. A prompt asking the model to behave is not a control.

The threat model

  • Direct prompt injection. A user tries to override your instructions. This matters for public-facing apps.

  • Indirect prompt injection. Instructions hide in content the agent reads: web pages, emails, PDFs, code comments, issue text, tool descriptions and tool output. This matters for every agent.

  • Malicious or changed tools. MCP tool descriptions can carry hidden instructions, and a server can change after you approve it.

  • Exfiltration channels. Any outbound path can leak data: a URL with data in its query string, a rendered Markdown image, a commit, an email, even a DNS lookup.

  • Supply chain. Model files, MCP servers, plugins, custom nodes, and agent configuration inside cloned repositories.

The lethal trifecta

Simon Willison's framing: an agent that has all three of the following can be tricked into leaking your data (Willison):

  1. Access to private data

  2. Exposure to untrusted content

  3. A way to communicate externally

Remove any one and that attack fails. For every agent you build, ask which leg you can cut.

AgentPrivate dataUntrusted contentOutbound channelSafer design
Coding agent on your repo with web accessYesYes: web pages, issues, dependenciesYesSandbox with an egress allowlist; no secrets in its environment
Email summarizerYesYesOnly if it can send or browseRead-only; never render links or remote images from mail
Research agent on the public webNoYesYesAcceptable while nothing private is in reach
Document Q&A on an offline machineYesPossiblyNoThe outbound leg is cut by design

Designs that resist injection

  • The dual-LLM pattern. A privileged model plans and calls tools but never sees untrusted text. A quarantined model handles untrusted text and can only return constrained values (Willison).

  • CaMeL. Google DeepMind's design has the privileged model write a program from the user's request alone. Untrusted data flows through it only as tagged values, and a policy checks where each tool call's data came from (CaMeL).

  • Plan, then execute. Fix the list of actions before the agent reads any untrusted content.

  • Least privilege. Scope tools to the task, make them read-only by default, and give write access to a branch rather than to main.

  • Approval at the boundary. Require a human for anything irreversible or outbound.

  • Treat model output as untrusted input to your own systems. Never pass it unchecked to a shell, SQL or eval, and never auto-render it as HTML in a privileged context.

Sandboxing agents

Claude Code's sandbox puts OS-level limits on shell commands and their child processes: file writes are confined and network access is limited to approved domains. Anthropic states that it is not a complete isolation boundary, since file tools, MCP servers and hooks run outside it (sandboxing). For unattended runs, or any run that bypasses permissions, use a disposable container or VM. Give it only the credentials the task needs and an egress allowlist.

Supply chain

  • Model files. Python pickle formats (.pt, .bin, .ckpt) can run arbitrary code when loaded. Prefer safetensors and GGUF, never set trust_remote_code=True for a repository you have not reviewed, and keep inference engines updated, since file parsers have had memory-safety bugs.

  • MCP servers and plugins. Install from trusted sources, pin versions and review changes on update. Known incidents include malicious MCP configurations planted in public repositories and a critical flaw in the widely used mcp-remote package (CVE-2025-6514).

  • Configuration in cloned repositories. .mcp.json, hook settings and CLAUDE.md files are all executable intent. Read them before you trust a folder.

  • ComfyUI custom nodes are arbitrary Python, and malicious nodes have been published. Install only maintained nodes from known authors.

Secrets and red-teaming

  • Secrets. Keep keys in environment variables or a secret manager. Give agents short-lived, narrowly scoped tokens, and use a separate API key with its own spend limit for each project.

  • Red-team your own agents. Plant an instruction in a test document, such as one telling the agent to print the contents of ~/.ssh. Confirm that the design stops it, not the model's good judgment.

#14. Local inference tuning

Measure first, fit as much as possible into VRAM, then trade context, precision and batch size against speed. For mixture-of-experts models, the decisive trick is to keep attention and shared weights on the GPU and put the experts in system RAM. Your RAM's bandwidth then sets the speed.

Measure

llama-bench -m model.gguf -ngl 99 -p 512 -n 128   # prompt processing (pp512) and generation (tg128), tokens/s

Compare the generation rate with the bandwidth ceiling: a 17.6 GB file on a 1,008 GB/s card caps at about 57 tokens per second, and good setups reach 60–80% of that. Benchmark at the context length you will actually use, because generation slows as the KV cache grows. Watch nvidia-smi for memory and power while it runs.

Expert offload, quantified

Each generated token reads the attention weights, any shared experts, and the k routed experts the router picks. With the routed experts in system RAM, time per token is roughly:

ttoken≈bytes read on the GPUβGPU+bytes read from RAMβRAMt_{\text{token}} \approx \frac{\text{bytes read on the GPU}}{\beta_{\text{GPU}}} + \frac{\text{bytes read from RAM}}{\beta_{\text{RAM}}}

Take gpt-oss-120b: 5.1B active parameters at about 4.25 bits read 2.7 GB per token, most of it routed experts. Dual-channel DDR5-6000 peaks at 96 GB/s and delivers perhaps 80 in practice. With about 2.3 GB of experts in RAM, that gives roughly 29 ms per token, a ceiling of about 30 tokens per second. Expect less in practice. As a sanity check, the same arithmetic for a DGX Spark (273 GB/s, all weights in one memory) gives a ceiling near 100. It measured 53 (jetsonhacks).

Three consequences follow:

  • RAM bandwidth sets the speed, so dual versus quad memory channels matters more than CPU model.

  • Prompt processing suffers more than generation, because the CPU computes the offloaded experts.

  • Find the cutoff by trial. Increase --n-cpu-moe until the model fits, and stop there.

vLLM for throughput

When several agents share one GPU, a batching server delivers several times the aggregate tokens per second of single-stream llama.cpp. This is the roofline batching effect from Chapter 4. vLLM runs under WSL2 on Windows (vLLM install):

vllm serve <an AWQ, GPTQ or FP8 model from Hugging Face> \
  --max-model-len 32768 --gpu-memory-utilization 0.90

Add --tensor-parallel-size 2 for two identical GPUs. llama.cpp is more forgiving with mixed cards.

Power, heat and noise

Decoding is memory-bound, so a GPU power cap set with nvidia-smi -pl costs little generation speed. Prefill and image generation are compute-bound and slow down more. Measure on your own card: run llama-bench at full power and at about 70% of the limit, and compare.

Multiple GPUs

The default layer split passes only activations between cards, so it works over ordinary PCIe with little penalty for single-stream generation. Row or tensor splitting needs fast interconnects. Budget for the power supply, slot spacing and airflow before buying a second card.

A tuning checklist

  1. Pick the largest model whose weights fit with about 2 GB to spare, plus the KV cache for your context.

  2. Put all layers on the GPU, turn on FlashAttention, and use an 8-bit KV cache if you need more context.

  3. For an MoE model larger than VRAM, raise --n-cpu-moe until it fits.

  4. Benchmark prefill and generation at your real context length, and compare with the bandwidth ceiling.

  5. For code-editing work, add speculative decoding and measure the acceptance rate.

  6. For several clients, use llama-server -np or vLLM.

#15. Fine-tuning in depth

LoRA adapts a model by learning a low-rank correction to selected weight matrices. QLoRA keeps the frozen base model in 4-bit memory, so a single 24 GB GPU can fine-tune models up to about 30B parameters. The data and the chat template decide the result far more than the hyperparameters.

LoRA, mathematically

W′=W+αr BA,B∈Rd×r,  A∈Rr×k,  r≪min⁡(d,k)W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BA,\qquad B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r},\; A\in\mathbb R^{r\times k},\; r \ll \min(d,k)

B starts at zero, so training begins exactly at the base model (LoRA). Each adapted matrix gains r(d + k) trainable parameters instead of d·k. For one 5,120 × 25,600 feed-forward matrix at r = 16, that is 491,520 parameters instead of 131 million, or 0.37%. Adapting all seven projection matrices in every layer of the 32B example adds 134 million parameters, 0.4% of the model. The method works because fine-tuning updates turn out to have low intrinsic rank, much as a low-order correction often captures most of a model error.

QLoRA and the memory budget

QLoRA stores the base weights in a 4-bit NormalFloat format, quantizes the quantization scales themselves, and pages optimizer states out of GPU memory under pressure. The LoRA weights train in 16-bit on top (QLoRA).

Data: where results are made

  • Use the model's own chat template, exactly as it will appear at inference (tokenizer.apply_chat_template). A mismatched template is the most common silent failure.

  • Compute the loss on the assistant's tokens only, masking out the prompt.

  • Prefer coverage to volume. 500–5,000 varied examples usually beat 100,000 repetitive ones. Include the hard cases, and include examples of declining if you want the model to decline.

  • Deduplicate, hold out about 10% for evaluation, and check for leakage between the two.

  • If a frontier model writes your training data, review a sample by hand and read the provider's terms first.

#17. Retrieval engineering

Retrieval quality is mostly settled before the LLM sees anything: by how documents are split, how chunks are described, and how candidates are found and ranked. Hybrid keyword-plus-vector search with reranking and context-enriched chunks cuts retrieval failures by about two-thirds compared with plain vector search. A small labeled set of queries tells you which change helped.

First, ask whether you need RAG

For a corpus under about 200,000 tokens, Anthropic recommends putting the whole thing in the prompt and caching it (Contextual Retrieval). Use retrieval for larger or frequently changing corpora.

Chunking

  • Split along structure: sections, headings, requirement IDs, functions. Fixed character counts cut ideas in half. 300–800 tokens with modest overlap is a reasonable start.

  • Keep metadata on every chunk: document, section path, page, revision, date. It serves both filtering and citations.

  • Extract tables and equations as text, as Markdown or LaTeX, and check the converter on your hardest pages.

  • Retrieve small, return large. Match on small chunks, then hand the model each chunk's parent section.

Embeddings

An embedding model encodes queries and chunks separately and compares them by cosine similarity. This is cheap and scales well, but it is approximate. When choosing a model, check:

  • Maximum input length and vector dimension, which drives storage.

  • Matryoshka training, which lets you truncate vectors to fewer dimensions at a modest loss (MRL).

  • Query and document prefixes: some models expect a different instruction on each side, so read the model card.

The weak spot is exact identifiers, such as part numbers, requirement IDs, function names and rare jargon. That is why keyword search stays in the loop.

Hybrid search with reciprocal rank fusion

Run BM25 keyword search and vector search in parallel, then merge the two result lists by rank:

RRF(d)=∑r ∈ rankers1k+rankr(d),k=60\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \,\in\, \text{rankers}} \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_r(d)},\qquad k = 60

Because fusion uses ranks rather than raw scores, the two systems' scores never need calibrating against each other.

Rerankers

A cross-encoder reranker reads the query and a candidate together and scores their relevance. It is far more accurate than embedding similarity and far slower, so apply it to the top 50–150 candidates and keep the best 5–20. Late-interaction models such as ColBERT sit between the two in cost and accuracy (ColBERT).

Contextual retrieval

Before indexing, have a cheap model write 50–100 tokens placing each chunk in its document. For example: "From the 2025 thermal test report, section 4.2: cold-soak results for unit B." Prepend that text to the chunk and build both the embedding and the BM25 index from the result. In Anthropic's tests, the top-20 retrieval failure rate fell:

  • 35% with contextual embeddings alone

  • 49% after adding contextual BM25

  • 67% with reranking on top, from 5.7% to 1.9%

With prompt caching, the one-time cost was $1.02 per million document tokens at 2024 prices (Anthropic).

Query-side techniques

  • Query rewriting: let the model turn a question into one or more search queries.

  • HyDE: embed a hypothetical answer instead of the question (HyDE).

  • Filter first on metadata such as document type, date and revision, then search.

  • Agentic retrieval: give the model search as a tool and let it iterate. This works best for questions that need several hops.

Measuring retrieval

Build 50–200 questions, each paired with the chunks that answer it, then measure:

Recall@k=1∣Q∣∑q∈Q1[an answering chunk is in the top k],MRR=1∣Q∣∑q∈Q1rankq\mathrm{Recall@}k = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q} \mathbb 1\big[\text{an answering chunk is in the top } k\big],\qquad \mathrm{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\frac{1}{\mathrm{rank}_q}

Recall@k matters most, since the model cannot use what was never retrieved. Use nDCG when relevance is graded rather than yes or no. Measure end-to-end answer accuracy separately (Chapter 9), so you can tell retrieval failures from generation failures.

When relationships matter

Some questions are about structure: "Which tests cover the requirements affected by this interface change?" Graph-based retrieval builds an entity-relation graph for this kind of question (GraphRAG), but it is expensive to extract from free text. If the structure already exists, such as a requirements-to-tests trace table, a SQL query tool for an agent often beats any vector database.

#18. Scientific machine learning for simulation engineers

Machine learning earns a place in simulation in two situations: a model will be queried many times, as in Monte Carlo campaigns, optimization, uncertainty quantification or real-time use; or part of the physics is unknown and data can fill the gap. Hybrid physics–ML models: learn the residual

x˙=fphys(x,u; θphys)+gψ(x,u)\dot x = f_{\text{phys}}(x, u;\, \theta_{\text{phys}}) + g_\psi(x, u)

Keep what you trust in f_phys: rigid-body dynamics, gravity, known actuator models. Let the network g_ψ learn what is missing, such as aerodynamic coefficient corrections, friction, or unmodeled sensor errors. The physics supplies structure and extrapolation, and the network only has to model a small correction.

  • Constrain the correction. Bound its output, enforce known symmetries, and penalize its magnitude.

  • Train it either by integrating trajectories and matching data, which needs a differentiable integrator, or by regressing derivatives estimated from the data.

Differentiable simulation

When the simulator is differentiable, gradients flow through whole rollouts. That turns system identification, trajectory optimization, sensitivity analysis and policy learning into gradient descent. It is the capability adjoint methods provide, automated. The tools:

  • JAX with Diffrax for ODEs

  • PyTorch with torchdiffeq

  • NVIDIA Warp, differentiable GPU kernels written in Python, and the Newton physics engine built on it

Gradients can explode through long or chaotic rollouts. Truncate the horizon or use multiple shooting.

Learned components in estimation

  • Learned noise models that feed covariances to a classical filter.

  • KalmanNet, a recurrent network that learns the Kalman gain inside the filter's structure, for models that are only partly known (KalmanNet).

  • Learned measurement models, such as a network that maps images to measurements, provided their covariances are calibrated.

Keep the classical consistency tests. NEES and NIS must still pass on held-out data, and a learned component that makes the filter overconfident is worse than none.

Uncertainty quantification

  • Deep ensembles. Train about five networks from different seeds; their spread approximates epistemic uncertainty (Lakshminarayanan et al.).

  • Conformal prediction. From a calibration set of n residuals, it gives intervals with guaranteed coverage, assuming only exchangeability (Angelopoulos and Bates):

q^=the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉n quantile of ∣yi−f(xi)∣,P(y∈[f(x)−q^,  f(x)+q^])≥1−α\hat q = \text{the } \tfrac{\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil}{n} \text{ quantile of } |y_i - f(x_i)|,\qquad P\big(y \in [f(x) - \hat q,\; f(x) + \hat q]\big) \ge 1-\alpha

  • Act on the uncertainty. When the surrogate is unsure, fall back to the high-fidelity simulator. This is the core of multi-fidelity schemes.

Verification and validation of learned models

  1. Define the operating envelope and sample it deliberately, with Latin hypercube or Sobol designs that include edges and corners.

  2. Hold out whole regions, not random points, to test extrapolation honestly.

  3. Compare against the truth model on the metrics that matter downstream, such as Monte Carlo statistics of miss distance, not just mean squared error.

  4. Check physics: conservation, symmetry, monotonicity and the stability of long rollouts.

  5. Detect out-of-distribution inputs at run time and fall back.

  6. Put data, code and weights under configuration management together, as for any model you would certify.

Evidence that it works at scale

Weather forecasting is the clearest case. GraphCast beat a leading operational forecast system on about 90% of 1,380 verification targets (GraphCast). ECMWF now runs its own learned system, AIFS, operationally (ECMWF). Both trained on decades of reanalysis data, and that volume of data is the precondition for this kind of result.

#19. Learning-based autonomy

Learned controllers and learned perception now ship in robots and vehicles, and nearly all of them are trained or validated in simulation. Success usually turns on two engineering problems rather than on the learning algorithm: crossing the sim-to-real gap, and bounding what a learned component is allowed to do.

Reinforcement learning for control

The policy maximizes expected discounted return. PPO, the workhorse algorithm, takes clipped policy-gradient steps on batches of simulated rollouts (PPO):

J(π)=Eπ[∑tγtr(st,at)],L=E[min⁡(ρtAt, clip⁡(ρt,1−ϵ,1+ϵ) At)],ρt=πθ(at∣st)πold(at∣st)J(\pi) = \mathbb E_\pi\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t r(s_t,a_t)\Big],\qquad \mathcal L = \mathbb E\big[\min\big(\rho_t A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,A_t\big)\big],\quad \rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}

Massively parallel GPU simulation, such as Isaac Lab running thousands of environments on one GPU, turns days of experience into minutes. Reward design is control design by other means. It carries the same Goodhart risk as Chapter 3: the policy will exploit any gap in the reward.

Imitation learning

Behavior cloning trains a policy directly on demonstrations. Its weakness is compounding error: once the policy drifts off the demonstrated states, it has never seen what to do. DAgger fixes this by collecting expert corrections in the states the policy actually visits (DAgger). Diffusion Policy generates whole action sequences with a diffusion model, which handles demonstrations that solve a task in several different ways (Diffusion Policy).

Vision-language-action models

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) extend a vision-language model to output robot actions. RT-2 showed that web-scale pretraining transfers to robot control (RT-2), and Physical Intelligence's π0 adds a flow-matching action head to a vision-language backbone (π0). These generalist policies improve quickly. For high-reliability tasks they currently work best as high-level planners over classical low-level control.

World models

A world model learns the dynamics so a policy can train on imagined rollouts; Dreamer is the best-known example (DreamerV3). Video world models such as NVIDIA Cosmos and Google DeepMind's Genie 3 generate future observations for training and testing. A learned world model is a surrogate, so it carries all of Chapter 18's validation obligations, and its errors compound over long imagined rollouts.

Neural sensor simulation

  • Reconstruction. NeRF and 3D Gaussian splatting turn captured images of a real scene into a renderable 3-D representation. This enables camera simulation that looks like the real world, from new viewpoints (NeRF, 3D Gaussian Splatting).

  • Closed-loop re-simulation. Autonomous-vehicle programs use reconstructed logs to replay a drive with a different ego trajectory and render what the sensors would have seen.

  • Other sensors. Lidar and radar need physically based extensions for ray drop, multipath and material reflectivity.

  • Variety from generative models. Generative models add weather and actors, but labels and physical consistency must come from the simulator or be validated separately.

Physics-based sensor models remain the reference for validating neural ones.

Assurance: a learned controller in a safe box

  • Runtime assurance (the Simplex pattern). A high-performance learned controller runs inside a monitored envelope. A verified safety controller takes over when the monitor predicts the envelope will be violated. ASTM F3269 codifies this approach for aircraft systems.

  • Control barrier functions act as a safety filter that changes the learned action as little as possible while keeping a safety function h(x) non-negative (Ames et al.):

asafe=arg⁡min⁡a∥a−alearned∥2s.t.h˙(x,a)≥−κ h(x)a_{\text{safe}} = \arg\min_a \|a - a_{\text{learned}}\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \dot h(x,a) \ge -\kappa\, h(x)

  • Validation at scale. Use scenario coverage of the operational design domain, falsification (optimization that searches for failures), and importance sampling for rare events.

  • Know what clean runs prove. With zero failures in n independent runs, the 95% upper bound on the failure probability is about 3/n, the rule of three. Three thousand clean Monte Carlo runs bound the failure rate only below 1 in 1,000.

#20. Capstone design: an agentic simulation lab

The design principle is a division of labor. The model decides what to run and explains the results. Deterministic code runs the simulations, computes the statistics and checks them. Nothing reaches the report without passing checks the agent cannot edit.

Failed checks loop back to the agent with the specific failure. Only results that pass the gate reach the report, and a subagent reviews the report in a fresh context.

A sketch of the idempotent submit, using the FastMCP interface from 101, Chapter 11:

import json, subprocess, uuid
from mcp.server.fastmcp import FastMCP

mcp = FastMCP("sim-tools")
JOBS: dict[str, dict] = {}

@mcp.tool()
def submit_sweep(configs: list[dict], seeds: list[int], request_id: str) -> dict:
    """Start a Monte Carlo sweep asynchronously. Idempotent: resubmitting a request_id
    returns the original job. Validate each config with validate_config first."""
    if request_id in JOBS:
        return JOBS[request_id]
    job_id = uuid.uuid4().hex[:8]
    proc = subprocess.Popen(["python", "run_sweep.py", "--job", job_id],
                            stdin=subprocess.PIPE, text=True)
    proc.stdin.write(json.dumps({"configs": configs, "seeds": seeds}))
    proc.stdin.close()
    JOBS[request_id] = {"job_id": job_id, "cases": len(configs) * len(seeds), "status": "running"}
    return JOBS[request_id]

In a real system, keep the job table in a database rather than in memory, so a restart cannot lose track of running jobs.

The verification gate

  1. Analytic cases run with every sweep: a vacuum ballistic case against its closed form, the unit norm of attitude quaternions, an energy budget with drag turned off.

  2. Golden regression. A reference configuration's statistics must match within tolerance.

  3. Statistical sufficiency. There must be enough cases to support the claim, with intervals or the rule of three (Chapter 19), and no NaNs or silently failed cases.

  4. The checker sits outside the agent's write permissions, through Claude Code deny rules or a separate CI job (Chapter 12).

Provenance

Every result records its configuration hash, seed, code commit, simulator and tool versions, and which checks it passed. The report cites a run ID for every number. A reviewer subagent with read-only tools then checks each number against the results store.

Permissions and cost

  • Models. Opus 5.5 plans; Haiku 5.5 subagents triage logs. Raw data stays out of the main context through summarize_results or programmatic tool calling.

  • Approval. A PreToolUse hook asks for your approval before any sweep above a compute budget.

  • Isolation. The simulator runs in a container, and the agent has no network access beyond the model API.

Evaluate the lab itself

Write 20–30 sweep requests with known correct outcomes. Include some the system should refuse or question, such as a configuration outside the validated envelope. Run each several times and score the plan, the numbers, whether the checks were honored, and the cost. This is Chapter 9 applied to your own agent.

#21. The frontier and how to judge it

The best-measured trend is steady. The length of task that agents finish half the time has doubled roughly every 89 days since 2024, and the best agents now pass that bar on tasks that take an expert 16 hours or more (METR). The unsolved problems are reliability, verification and cost, so the engineering in this guide matters more as models improve, not less.

Open problems

  • Verification at scale. Tests, types, review agents and formal methods help, but checking a 10,000-line change remains the bottleneck.

  • Long-horizon memory and credit assignment, for agents that work over days.

  • Calibration: models that reliably know when they do not know.

  • Faithful reasoning, where a model's stated reasoning matches its actual computation (Chapter 7).

  • Prompt injection, which has no complete fix, only designs that contain it (Chapter 11).

  • Dual-use capabilities. Tiered safeguards, such as Fable versus Mythos, and verified-access programs show labs treating some capabilities as hazardous.

How to judge a claim

  1. Measurement or demo? Look for held-out tasks with error bars.

  2. Under what conditions? Check the harness, the effort setting and the number of attempts.

  3. On your work? Run your own eval before forming an opinion.

  4. At what cost per finished task?

  5. Who benefits from the claim? Vendor tables are selective; independent measurements and your own eval are not.

#22. A 201 project track

Eight projects, each a weekend or two, turn this guide into working skill. They run roughly in order of the chapters, and each has a concrete acceptance test. Tick them off as you go.

  • 1. Build a GPT from scratch with nanoGPT, or the end-to-end nanochat, which adds fine-tuning and RL (Chapters 1–3). Done when your hand-derived parameter count and FLOPs per token match the code, and you can explain every line.

  • 2. Measure your GPU's roofline (Chapters 4 and 14). Done when llama-bench results at three quantizations of one model sit within 30% of the bandwidth ceiling, plotted against it.

  • 3. Build an eval harness with error bars (Chapter 9). Done when 100 or more tasks, three models and three runs each produce a paired comparison with 95% intervals.

  • 4. Wrap a simulator in an MCP server (Chapters 8, 11 and 20). Done when it passes the MCP Inspector, submits are idempotent, errors are actionable, and Claude Code uses it to run a sweep.

  • 5. Fine-tune with QLoRA and compare against prompting (Chapter 15). Done when the fine-tuned model beats its base by more than the error bars on a held-out set, with a frontier model's score alongside for comparison.

  • 6. Train an image LoRA and script a sweep (Chapter 16). Done when a 50-image sweep runs through ComfyUI's API with every parameter logged.

  • 7. Build a neural surrogate with calibrated uncertainty (Chapter 18). Done when a surrogate of a projectile or 6-DOF simulation reaches its target conformal coverage on a held-out region, and its speedup is measured.

  • 8. Train a control policy with domain randomization (Chapter 19). Done when you can show success rates with and without randomization under perturbed dynamics, using Isaac Lab or MuJoCo.
