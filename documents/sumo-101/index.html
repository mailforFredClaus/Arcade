Your host, Konishiki. Born in Hawaii, he was the first non-Japanese-born wrestler to reach ōzeki, sumo's second-highest rank. At his peak he weighed close to 290 kg (about 630 lb), the heaviest top-division wrestler on record. He won three top-division championships before retiring in 1997.

How it differs from sumo in Japan. Pro sumo runs six 15-day tournaments a year, and each wrestler fights once a day. This is an exhibition: rituals are shortened, there are fewer officials, and wrestlers fight several times in one night. The rules and the physics are the same. On past tours, the six wrestlers had 3 to 14 years of professional experience.

#Sumo in 60 seconds

Two wrestlers try to force each other out of a 4.55 m (15 ft) ring, or make the other touch the ground with anything but the soles of his feet. Most bouts are over in under 10 seconds.

  • No weight classes. A 100 kg (220 lb) wrestler can face one twice his size, so speed and technique regularly beat mass.

  • It started as religion. Sumo grew out of Shinto rituals performed to entertain the gods and pray for good harvests. Much of the ceremony you'll see is still about purifying the ring.

  • It turned professional over 300 years ago. Paid tournaments took shape in the Edo period (1600s onward). Today the Japan Sumo Association runs all professional sumo.

  • It's a way of life. Pro wrestlers (rikishi) live, eat and train together in stables, wear a topknot, and dress traditionally in public.

#How to win

You lose the instant any part of your body except the soles of your feet touches the ground, or any part of you touches the ground outside the ring. Everything else is detail.

SituationResult
A knee, hand, fingertip, or even the topknot touches the clayLoses
A foot touches the sand outside the straw balesLoses
A foot balanced on top of the straw bale (tawara)Still in: the bale is the edge
A heel hanging in the air past the baleStill in, until it touches down
Both fall togetherWhoever lost balance first (the "dead body") loses
The belt comes offLoses (very rare)

Legal: open-hand slaps and shoves, grabbing the belt, trips, throws, and sidestepping a charge.

Forbidden (kinjite): closed-fist punches, pulling hair, eye-gouging, choking, kicking the chest or stomach, grabbing the groin part of the belt, bending fingers back, and boxing both ears at once. Konishiki demonstrates some of these on stage.

Officials (in Japan). The referee (gyōji) points his war fan (gunbai) toward the winner's side. Five ringside judges can challenge the call; after a huddle (mono-ii) they confirm it, reverse it, or order a rematch (torinaoshi). There is no time limit, but very few bouts pass a minute.

#The ritual before the clash

In Japan, top-division wrestlers spend up to four minutes on ritual for a few seconds of fighting, and every step has a purpose. Expect a shortened version on stage, in roughly this order:

  1. Shiko (the leg stomp). Each wrestler lifts one leg high and slams it down, then the other. It drives evil spirits from the ring and loosens the hips.

  2. Chikara-mizu (power water). He rinses his mouth with water and wipes his face with paper, a purification step.

  3. Shio-maki (the salt throw). A handful of salt is scattered across the ring to purify it. Some wrestlers toss a pinch; crowd favorites throw a huge arc.

  4. Chiri-chōzu (showing empty hands). Squatting, he claps, spreads his arms palms up, then turns them down: an old way of showing he carries no weapon.

  5. Shikiri (the staredown). Both crouch behind white lines 70 cm apart, glare, then stand and walk back for more salt. They repeat this to build tension until time is up.

  6. Tachiai (the charge). There is no starting signal. Both put their fists to the clay and launch when they mutually decide to. A mistimed start (matta) is called back and redone.

Watch the tachiai above everything else. The wrestler who gets lower and moves first usually controls the bout. A henka, sidestepping the charge so the opponent stumbles past, is legal but considered cheap, and crowds often groan.

In Japan, top-division wrestlers also parade into the ring in embroidered silk aprons (keshō-mawashi), and a yokozuna performs his own entrance ritual wearing a thick white rope.

#Winning techniques to watch for

The Japan Sumo Association recognizes 82 official winning techniques (kimarite), but a handful decide most bouts. Learn the nine below and you'll recognize most finishes; the technique is announced after each bout.

Two styles. Watch the hands in the first second:

  • Pushers (oshi-zumo) keep their arms extended, thrusting and slapping at the chest and throat to drive the opponent back without grabbing the belt.

  • Belt fighters (yotsu-zumo) close to chest-to-chest range and grab the mawashi. Both hands inside the opponent's arms (morozashi) is a dominant grip; an outside grip (uwate) powers throws.

Yorikiri and oshidashi alone end roughly half of top-division bouts (approximate figure). Rare finishes like utchari are what make crowds roar. A bout can also end with no technique at all, for example when a wrestler steps out on his own.

#The professional world

Rank decides everything in pro sumo, from pay to who does the chores, and it moves after every tournament. Japan holds six 15-day tournaments a year: January, May and September in Tokyo, March in Osaka, July in Nagoya, and November in Fukuoka.

A winning record (8 or more wins out of 15) moves a wrestler up; a losing one moves him down. Ōzeki promotion usually takes about 33 wins over three tournaments at the two ranks below, and yokozuna usually requires back-to-back championships as ōzeki.

#The wrestlers

Professional wrestlers live inside a strict, centuries-old system from the day they join, often straight out of school.

Size. Top-division wrestlers average roughly 160 kg (350 lb), but the range is wide: some weigh under 120 kg, a few over 200 kg (approximate figures). Most of it sits on a huge, low base, and many can do the full splits; flexibility drills are part of daily training.

Stable life. Every wrestler belongs to a stable (heya) run by a retired wrestler, the stablemaster (oyakata). A typical day:

  • Wake around 5 a.m. and train on an empty stomach for several hours.

  • Eat a big late-morning meal, usually chanko-nabe (a protein-heavy hot pot), then nap, which helps build weight.

  • Juniors cook, clean, and serve as attendants to the stable's ranked wrestlers.

Names. Wrestlers fight under ring names (shikona), often sharing a character with their stable. Sadogatake stable's wrestlers start with "Koto," as in Kotozakura.

Hair. All wear a topknot (chonmage), styled with scented wax by the stable's hairdresser. Wrestlers in the top two divisions wear an elaborate ginkgo-leaf version (ōichō-mage) on bout days. The topknot is cut off at a retirement ceremony.

The belt (mawashi). One strip of heavy cloth, roughly 7 to 9 m (23 to 30 ft) long, wound around the waist several times. The stiff strings hanging in front (sagari) are decorative and often fall out mid-bout, with no penalty.

#How to enjoy it live

Arrive early, watch the first second of every bout, and bring a question for the Q&A.

Before you go (from past tours; check your ticket for this weekend's details):

  • Friday and Saturday shows are 21+; Sunday is all ages. Bring ID.

  • Seating surrounds a square ring, and general seats have been first come, first served. Doors have opened an hour before showtime.

Five things to watch in every bout:

  1. The charge: who gets lower and moves first?

  2. The hands: pushing at the chest, or reaching for the belt?

  3. The grip: does anyone get both hands inside?

  4. The edge: watch the feet on the straw bales; comebacks happen there.

  5. The finish: call the technique before it's announced.

Cheering. Sumo crowds are loud: shout a wrestler's name, applaud the big salt throws, and groan at a henka. Just skip the Japanese habit of throwing cushions.

Questions for the Q&A:

  • How much do you eat on a training day?

  • What was the hardest part of stable life?

  • What's your favorite winning technique, and why?

  • What does the tachiai feel like?

  • How do you train flexibility?

If you're hooked. The next pro tournament, the Kyushu basho in Fukuoka, runs November 8 to 22, 2026. NHK World posts daily English highlights, and there's an official Grand Sumo app.
